Day 3 · Afternoon · CAP Phase 2 · CCP 35% / CCA 40%

Evidence: Adequacy, Sufficiency, and Collection

The conceptual hinge of the workshop. Everything so far has been about what applies and where. This is about proof — specifically the gap between an organization having a document and an organization doing the thing. Most people entering CMMC work close that gap without noticing. Anyone who leaves this session still equating documentation with compliance does not recover tomorrow.

Day 3 — PM ~3 hours Highest exam weight Tool: Evidence Triage

Regulatory status as of 16 November 2026: Phase 2 is suspended; check again before you teach.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to…

  • Distinguish adequacy from sufficiency and apply both tests to a real artifact. Bloom: Analyze
  • Decompose a NIST SP 800-171 requirement into its assessment objectives and identify what each objective actually demands. Bloom: Analyze
  • Select the appropriate assessment method — examine, interview, or test — and the assessment object for a given objective, and say what each method can and cannot establish. Bloom: Apply
  • Apply the CMMC sampling requirements for depth and coverage. Bloom: Apply
  • Construct an evidence request list specific enough that an organization could act on it without asking a question. Bloom: Create
  • Evaluate an artifact and state, with reasons, whether it is adequate, sufficient, both, or neither — and what would change the verdict. Bloom: Evaluate

1

Objectives, not requirements

You do not assess 110 requirements. You assess the assessment objectives those requirements decompose into — roughly 320 across Level 2, enumerated in NIST SP 800-171A — and the arithmetic is unforgiving.

There is no partial credit

Every assessment objective under a requirement must be MET, or NOT APPLICABLE, for the parent requirement to be MET. One objective unmet makes the whole requirement NOT MET.

This is the single most consequential mechanic in Level 2 assessment. It is why an organization that has “mostly” implemented a control has not implemented it, and why an assessor who evaluates at the requirement level rather than the objective level produces determinations that cannot be defended.

Take AC.L2-3.1.1 — limit system access to authorized users, processes acting on behalf of authorized users, and devices. It looks like one idea. It is not: authorized users are identified; processes acting on behalf of authorized users are identified; devices authorized to connect are identified; and system access is limited to each of the three. Evidence that establishes the first three and nothing about the fourth leaves the requirement NOT MET.

2

Adequacy and sufficiency

The sentence this session is built on

Adequate evidence shows the thing exists and matches what the requirement intends. Sufficient evidence shows it is implemented, consistently followed, and operating.

A policy stating that multi-factor authentication is required is adequate. A configuration export plus a sample of enrolled accounts is sufficient. Someone telling you it is enforced is neither.

AdequacySufficiency
The questionDoes this evidence exist, and does it match what the requirement intends?Does it show the control is implemented, consistently followed, and operating?
A policy documentUsually adequate — it exists and states the intentAlmost never sufficient on its own
A configuration exportAdequate if it addresses the objectiveSufficient if it covers the assets in scope and is current
A screenshotAdequate for what is on screenSufficient only if the scope of what it shows matches the scope of the requirement
A verbal assuranceNeitherNeither
What to ask next“What is this a document about?”“How would I know this is still true, and true everywhere?”

The three failures you will see most

  1. The artifact exists but does not address the objective — a policy about passwords offered for a requirement about session termination.
  2. The artifact addresses the objective but covers only part of the scope — real evidence for one system, offered for an environment containing seven.
  3. The artifact is a claim rather than a record — an email saying the thing happens, a spreadsheet built from memory, a person of unquestioned competence saying “yes, we do that.”
Exam tip

Adequacy is a property of the artifact, not of the organization. A well-drafted policy in a non-compliant company is still adequate, and being able to say so is the discrimination that separates a Competent assessor from one who marks everything “neither” because the company is a mess.

3

Methods and objects

Three methods, and each pairs with the kind of object it can actually speak to.

MethodWhat it doesWhat it establishesWhat it cannot establish
ExamineReview, inspect, study, or analyze an object — specifications, mechanisms, activities, or documentsThat something exists, and what it says or how it is configuredThat anyone follows it, or that it was true yesterday
InterviewDiscuss with individuals or groupsWhat people understand, what they say they do, and where practice diverges from documentationAnything, on its own. An interview generates leads, not conclusions
TestExercise an object under specified conditions and compare actual to expected behaviourThat the control operates as intended, right nowThat it operated last quarter, or across assets you did not test
Depth and coverage

The CAP requires a nonstatistical sampling approach, and requires the assessment team to apply the FOCUSED value for both depth and coverage across all Level 2 security requirements, as applicable. The team should increase the sample when it encounters questionable, insufficient, or inadequate evidence.

Two practical consequences. Where an organization has multiple CAGE codes, all of them must be accounted for in the sampling approach. Where it has multiple physical locations — as Meridian does — the sampling should consider whether the locations use different physical controls and whether scans and boundaries cover all of them.

Note the word the Assessment Guide uses: potential assessment methods and objects. The guide lists what could establish an objective, not what must. Choosing among them is your judgment, and exercising it is what you are being trained to do.

4

Five artifacts, evaluated

These are the artifacts Meridian supplied. Nothing has been edited to make them better or worse than they are. Work each one before reading the verdict.

Artifact 1 — Access control policy excerpt

Supplied by Trevor Nakamura as a PDF. Document properties: created 14 March 2021, last modified 14 March 2021. Four clauses on need-to-know, account creation and disablement, administrative privilege restriction, and periodic review by the IT Manager. Approved by the CEO.

Adequate. Not sufficient. It exists, it is approved, and clauses 4.1 and 4.3 address the intent of AC.L2-3.1.1, 3.1.2, and 3.1.5. It is not sufficient because there is no access control matrix, access is managed from the IT Manager's memory, a QuickBooks administrator account belonging to a departed employee has been active for fourteen months, and the Contracts Manager does not know who can reach the folder she asked to be restricted. Clause 4.4 promises periodic review; no record of a review exists.
What would change it: an access control matrix; an account listing with privilege levels reconciled against it; dated review records naming the reviewer; termination records showing accounts disabled on separation.

Artifact 2 — Conditional Access configuration

A screenshot of the Microsoft 365 admin centre. Policy “Require MFA for all users,” State: On, Users: All users (163), Cloud apps: All, Conditions: Any location, Grant: Require MFA — with the shared service account mpds-admin@ listed under Excluded. Tenant identifier visible and matching. Exported three days before the request.

The hardest artifact in the set. This is genuinely good evidence — current, scoped, attributable, recent. And it does not carry IA.L2-3.5.3, because the requirement applies across the assessment scope and the scope is not Microsoft 365. The PLM, the file server, domain controller RDP, the SSL VPN, QuickBooks, and the backup NAS all authenticate with single-factor credentials. Evidence covering one system out of seven establishes the objective for one system out of seven.
Two secondary observations: a screenshot shows the policy exists and is enabled; it does not show exclusions, which is where Conditional Access policies usually leak — the full policy export is the better artifact. And the assessor should test with a sample account rather than rely on configuration alone.

Hold this distinction

The artifact is excellent and the requirement is still NOT MET. An assessor who cannot praise good evidence and still find the requirement unmet will be argued out of findings in the field.

Artifact 3 — Email from the IT Manager

“Logging is turned on across the board — Windows event logs on all the servers and the FortiGate firewall logs everything. I check them regularly and we have not seen anything unusual. If something looked wrong I would know about it. I have been doing this here for nine years.”

Neither. It is an assertion that arrived by email, which does not make it a record. An interview response is a lead to be corroborated, not a conclusion, and this is an interview response in written form.

Two rhetorical moves are worth naming. “I have been doing this here for nine years” is an appeal to authority. “We have not seen anything unusual” is an appeal to the absence of evidence — and with no review records, no aggregation, and no SIEM, Meridian has no capability to have seen anything unusual. The absence of observed events is not evidence of absence when nothing is looking.

Artifact 4 — Risk Assessment Report (POL-006)

Supplied by Diana Kowalczyk. The document register records it as last performed June 2023 and flags it “Overdue.” Six risks, each with likelihood, impact, a level, a remediation state and an owner.

Adequate. Not sufficient. Unlike most artifacts in this case it is the right kind of document, it identifies real risks specific to this environment, and it assigns ownership. Meridian has genuinely done a risk assessment. But the requirement is to assess risk periodically, and this was performed once, 19 months ago, with no next review scheduled — so there is no periodicity to evidence. Two of the six risks record no remediation at all and one records “planned” with no date.

The observation that separates Competent from Assessor-Ready: read the register against your own findings. RISK-01, RISK-02 and RISK-03 describe, in the company's own words, three of the failures you are about to write up — unpatched workstations, MSP privileged access, stale VPN accounts. Meridian identified them, rated them Critical and High, assigned owners, and did nothing for 19 months. That is not an unknown risk. It is an accepted one that nobody was authorised to accept.

Artifact 5 — The POA&M

Supplied by Diana Kowalczyk as an .xlsx attachment. Six items. Four carry no target date, none carries a named individual owner — the owner column reads “IT” or “Contracts” — none records resources, and no item has ever been closed. No version history. No AO signature. Last modified 18 months ago. Asked where the master copy lives, she states it is in her OneDrive.

Neither — and the artifact is itself a finding. A plan of action must identify the weakness, the corrective action, the resources required, the milestones and the completion dates. This does not match what the requirement intends, so it is not adequate.

Where an artifact lives can be worse than what it says

The master copy is in a personal OneDrive on a commercial tenant, not in the authorised GCC High environment. A document enumerating every unremediated security weakness in a defense contractor is precisely what an adversary would target, and it is being kept outside the accredited boundary — which also contradicts the SSP's own boundary statement.

This is the clearest example in the case of a finding that has nothing to do with the content of the document. Ask where evidence lives, not only what it says.

Consistency check

You will need the POA&M again on Friday, when the eligibility rules in 32 CFR §170.21 turn on what a plan of action may and may not carry. Note now that a POA&M is a regulated instrument with a defined content standard, not a spreadsheet of intentions — and that an assessor who accepts this one as evidence of anything will have a difficult Friday.

5

Building the request list

An evidence request list is a working instrument, not a wish list. Each line names four things.

ElementWhy it matters
The artifactSpecific enough to be retrieved. “Access control documentation” is not a request; “the current Conditional Access policy export from the Microsoft 365 tenant” is.
The methodExamine, interview, or test. This tells the organization what form the evidence has to take.
The objective it servesWhich assessment objective this is meant to establish. If you cannot name one, delete the line.
The custodianWho holds it. At Meridian this is frequently one person, which is itself a finding.
Where assessments go wrong quietly

A vague request produces a pile of documents, a week of delay, and an assessment team that starts accepting whatever arrived because the schedule is slipping. Nobody decides to lower the standard; it lowers itself. Specificity up front is the control.

6

The verdict is the least interesting part

Most assessors reach similar verdicts on these five artifacts. What separates them is the last clause — what would change it.

An assessor who can say “this is adequate but not sufficient, and here is precisely what would make it sufficient” has given an organization something it can act on, without ever telling it what to do. That is the line you will walk for the rest of your career, and it is the reason every verdict in this workshop carries a what would change it field.

The habit

Naming what evidence is missing is assessment. Telling Meridian how to produce it is not. Name the gap precisely enough that the fix is obvious without ever being stated.

The Meridian case

Meridian · Day 3 Afternoon · Deliverable D5 · Fictional case

Triage five artifacts and build the request list

The Evidence Triage tool presents the five artifacts one at a time, in full, exactly as they were supplied. For each you name the assessment objective it is offered against, render a verdict — adequate, sufficient, both, or neither — give the reasoning, and say what would change the verdict. The tool then shows the model evaluation with the reasoning exposed, including the observations that separate a Competent verdict from an Assessor-Ready one.

Part B is the request list: fifteen requirements, from AC.L2-3.1.1 through SC.L2-3.13.1, each needing one or more lines naming a retrievable artifact, a method, the objective it serves, and the custodian. The tool rejects lines that name a topic rather than an artifact.

Every verdict must say what would change it. A verdict without that clause is incomplete, and the tool will tell you so.

For faculty

Faculty teaching segment

  • Signature assignment: Students evaluate five fictional artifacts (the D5 deliverable): the objective each is offered against, a verdict (adequate, sufficient, both, neither), the reasoning, and what would change it; then they write a request list that names retrievable artifacts.
  • Rubric criteria:
    • Each artifact is tied to a specific assessment objective
    • Adequacy and sufficiency are both applied, separately
    • Every verdict says what evidence would change it
    • Request lines name an artifact, a method, the objective, and the custodian, never a topic
  • Lab idea: Use the Evidence Triage tool’s five artifacts; have half the class argue each verdict and half argue against it.
  • Course fit: IT audit, security assessment and testing, GRC.
🎓 Teaching noteOne artifact separates Competent from Assessor-Ready: excellent technical evidence for a requirement that is still NOT MET. Watch for students who accept it outright and students who reject it outright.

CMMC Practice · A6⏱ 35 min

Evidence That Holds Up

Match evidence to methods and judge adequacy and sufficiency.

Assessment Lab · B5⏱ 60 min

Evidence Triage

Triage five artifacts, then write a request list that names retrievable artifacts.

Knowledge Check

Test your judgment

Ten questions in CCP style. Read every rationale.

Q1.A requirement has five assessment objectives. Evidence establishes four; the fifth is not addressed by anything supplied. What is the determination?

Question 1 options
Correct: C. Every objective must be MET or NOT APPLICABLE for the parent requirement to be MET. Four out of five is NOT MET, and the finding names the objective that failed. Why the others fall short: A There is no MET-with-weakness determination. A noted weakness at objective level is what NOT MET means.; B Preponderance has no place in this determination. Importing it from other assurance regimes is a reliable way to produce findings that collapse.; D Deferral is not a determination. You may request more evidence during the assessment; at determination time the answer is MET, NOT MET, or NOT APPLICABLE.

Q2.Meridian supplies a current Conditional Access export showing MFA enforced for all 163 users on all cloud apps, with one shared Global Admin service account excluded. The SSL VPN has no MFA. How do you evaluate the artifact against IA.L2-3.5.3?

Question 2 options
Correct: D. Evidence covering one system out of seven establishes the objective for one system out of seven. Praising the artifact and finding the requirement unmet are not in tension — an assessor who cannot do both will be argued out of findings. Why the others fall short: A It is all of those things for Microsoft 365. The requirement applies across the assessment scope, and the scope is not Microsoft 365.; B Rejecting good evidence because the requirement fails is the mirror-image error. Adequacy is a property of the artifact.; C The other systems are not a separate requirement. They are within the scope of this one, and the determination has to reflect that.

Q3.Which best describes what an interview can establish on its own?

Question 3 options
Correct: C. An interview establishes what people understand and where practice diverges from documentation. It is a lead, not a conclusion. Why the others fall short: A Competence and honesty are not evidence. The assessments that go wrong go wrong with likeable people.; B Existence of a document is established by examining it, not by being told about it.; D Nothing establishes past operation from a conversation. Even a test only establishes current operation.

Q4.An IT manager emails: "Logging is on everywhere and I check them regularly. If something looked wrong I would know. I've been doing this nine years." Evaluate against AU.L2-3.3.1 and 3.3.5.

Question 4 options
Correct: A. With no review records, no aggregation, and no SIEM, the organization has no capability to have seen anything unusual. Absence of observed events is not evidence of absence when nothing is looking. Why the others fall short: B An assertion in writing is still an assertion. The medium does not convert a claim into a record.; C Close, and it misses that the artifact does not meet the adequacy test at all. It is not a document about log review; it is a statement that log review happens.; D It is not evidence for either. 3.3.1 requires records created and retained; 3.3.5 requires correlation across sources.

Q5.Which evidence request line would you accept?

Question 5 options
Correct: D. Artifact, method, objective, custodian. An OSC could satisfy it without a phone call, and the mention of exclusions closes the gap a screenshot leaves open. Why the others fall short: A A topic, not an artifact. It produces a pile of documents and a week of delay.; B Names an objective, not a retrievable artifact, and does not say what form the evidence should take.; C The request that guarantees you receive a management summary of a report the organization does not hold.

Q6.Meridian's POA&M is a spreadsheet held in the CISO's personal OneDrive rather than in the GCC High tenant. Beyond its content, what does the assessor record?

Question 6 options
Correct: B. Two findings in one: the storage location itself, and the inconsistency with the SSP. Where an artifact lives can be a more serious finding than what it says — ask where evidence lives, not only what it says. Why the others fall short: A It is a boundary violation. The authorised environment for this company's sensitive material is the GCC High tenant, and this is not in it.; C That is remediation advice. Naming the gap is assessment; specifying the fix is not.; D It can and must be considered — as evidence about the company. Refusing to look at it would lose the finding.

Q7.What sampling values does the CAP require for Level 2 assessments?

Question 7 options
Correct: D. Increased where the team encounters questionable, insufficient, or inadequate evidence. Multiple CAGE codes must all be accounted for, and multiple locations considered for differing physical controls. Why the others fall short: A The CAP requires a nonstatistical approach. Statistical sampling language is imported from financial audit.; B Not required and not feasible. The sampling requirement exists precisely because full coverage is not the standard.; C Judgment applies to method selection, not to the depth and coverage values, which the CAP specifies.

Q8.An asset inventory lists "Laptops / desktops 41; Service accounts 3." Against CM.L2-3.4.1, this is:

Question 8 options
Correct: C. Not adequate because it does not match the intent; not sufficient because it was compiled from memory, is undated, and carries no baseline configurations, which is the other half of the requirement. Why the others fall short: A The category of document is right and the content does not address the objective. That distinction is the whole of adequacy.; B Dates and owners would not fix it. It omits every asset that holds CUI.; D It does not even establish the endpoint portion — there is no identification of components, only a count.

Q9.A policy is well drafted, approved by the CEO, and directly addresses the requirement's intent, but the organization demonstrably does not follow it. How is the artifact evaluated?

Question 9 options
Correct: B. It exists and matches what the requirement intends — adequate. It does not show the control is implemented, consistently followed, and operating — not sufficient. The requirement is NOT MET and the artifact is still adequate. Why the others fall short: A The most common error in the set. Adequacy is a property of the artifact, not of the organization.; C This combination is close to impossible. Sufficiency presupposes the evidence addresses the objective.; D Non-compliance is not a separate finding. It is what makes the evidence insufficient.

Q10.What must every verdict in an evidence log include beyond the verdict itself?

Question 10 options
Correct: A. An assessor who can say precisely what would make an artifact sufficient has given the organization something it can act on without ever telling it what to do. A verdict without that clause is incomplete. Why the others fall short: B Severity attaches to findings, and it follows from the requirement and the exposure, not from the evidence evaluation.; C That is remediation advice. Naming what is missing is assessment; telling the OSC how to produce it is not.; D Useful for records, and not the substantive element the evaluation turns on.
