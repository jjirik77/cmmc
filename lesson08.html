Day 5 · Morning · CAP Phases 2–3 · The mock assessment

Conducting the Assessment and Reporting Findings

The summative session. No lecture. Eight months have passed inside the case: the last C3PAO declined the engagement and — correctly — gave Meridian no help getting ready. The company hired a consultant, worked its plan, spent about $210,000, and engaged a different firm. You are that firm. Some of what you are about to read is real. Some of it is a document produced to close a line on a plan.

A

Learning objectives

By the end of this session you will be able to:

  • ExecuteExecute a Level 2 assessment against a supplied evidence package under time constraint.
  • RunRun the in-brief, daily checkpoint, and out-brief structure that CAP Phases 2 and 3 require.
  • ConductConduct interviews that elicit evidence rather than confirmation.
  • WriteWrite an evidence request that returns what you actually need.
  • RenderRender final determinations and identify the findings that follow from them.
  • ProduceProduce a findings report that is defensible against the Assessment Guide when challenged.

Key terminology

Click a card to reveal the definition. These are the terms the CCP and CCA exams use precisely, and the terms practitioners most often use loosely.

1

Two rules for the session

Non-negotiable

You may not tell Meridian how to fix anything. Not in the checkpoint, not in an interview, not in the out-brief, and not when the chief executive asks you directly and reasonably. She will.

You may not speculate about the outcome. Not “this is looking good,” not “you are close.” The CAP prohibits it in Phase 1 and the reasoning holds throughout: an assessment result is a determination, not a running commentary.

2

Running the in-brief

The CAP sets out what the Lead CCA must address at minimum. Your in-brief is assessed on whether you covered it.

  1. Introduce the assessment team and invite the OSC to introduce its people.
  2. Confirm the CMMC assessment scope.
  3. Explain the Level 2 assessment procedures under 32 CFR §170.17(c).
  4. Review the assessment schedule.
  5. Reconfirm the absence of, or disclose, any organizational or individual conflicts of interest.
  6. Inform the OSC of its right to appeal and describe the C3PAO's appeals process.
  7. Invite questions.

Minutes or a detailed summary must be kept. Nominate someone to take them before you start.

The checkpoint

A daily checkpoint with the OSC point of contact is required at the end of each assessment day: progress, challenges, and what you need next. It is not a negotiation and it is not a preview of the result.

The OSC will push back, and pushing back is legitimate — an organization that accepts every preliminary finding without question is not paying attention. Your job is to distinguish new information, which should change your call, from pressure, which should not.

The pattern to look for

This is a company that has genuinely improved, which makes it a harder assessment than the one you scoped on Wednesday. Most of these documents are real work. The failures are now in the joins: a CRM that allocates a duty nobody performed, a POA&M whose completions are unevidenced, an SSP that describes a designed environment rather than the operating one, and a tabletop that exercises a scenario the company has never faced while ignoring two it has.

A documentation-led assessment will pass Meridian. Only reading the documents against each other, and against what people tell you, will not.

4

Interviews that elicit rather than confirm

Six people are available and you will not reach all of them. Choosing who to interview is an assessment decision and it is assessed as one. Ask yourself who can tell you something no document can.

Everyone is cooperative and nobody is lying. They will answer the question you asked. Ask a question that invites agreement and you will get agreement, and you will have learned nothing while believing you learned something.

Instead ofAsk
“You review the audit logs monthly, correct?”“Walk me through the last review you did. What did you open?”
“Is MFA enabled?”“Take me system by system. What happens when you sign in to the PLM?”
“Do you have an offboarding process?”“The last person who left — tell me what happened to their accounts, and how you know.”
“Is the MSP access controlled?”“Walk me through what happens when a ClearPath technician needs to log in. Who approves it, what account do they use, and where would I see a record of it afterwards?”

Who is in the building

  • Diana Kowalczyk, CISO — owns the SSP, the policies and the POA&M. Competent, candid, and has been asking for budget for two years. She will tell you what is not fixed if you ask her plainly, and she is the reason anything improved at all.
  • Trevor Nakamura, IT Administrator — built and runs the environment. Cooperative and quietly proud. Believes that having a document means the control is implemented, and will say “it is in the SSP” as though that settles a question.
  • Priya Mehta, Contract Manager — precise, tracks DFARS clauses on the three prime and subcontract vehicles rigorously. Has never applied the same discipline downward to suppliers, and does not know what a Customer Responsibility Matrix commits the company to.
  • Renata Bauer, HR Director — organized about the things anyone has asked her to keep records of. The offboarding process is email-based and she has never been told that VPN deprovisioning is missing from it.
  • Gregory Farris, FSO and Physical Security Manager — runs badge access, visitor logs and media destruction at Dayton, and does it well. Has never visited Huntsville.
  • Marcus Ellison, President and CEO, Authorizing Official — warm, direct, commercially focused, genuinely likeable. He has spent real money on this. He will apply pressure without meaning it as pressure, which is what makes him the hardest interview in the room.
The choice that separates teams

Six people, forty minutes, and you will not reach them all. Choosing who to interview is an assessment decision and it is assessed as one.

Most teams pick Nakamura and Kowalczyk, because they own the systems and the documents. The higher-yield choices are usually Bauer — who can tell you exactly what the offboarding process does and does not include, which is the root cause of the finding that started this whole engagement — and Farris, who will tell you without prompting that he has never seen the Huntsville site, and that the escort policy for MSP technicians is something he does rather than something written down.

Ellison is the ethics test

At some point he will ask, warmly and reasonably: “Just tell me what you need to see and we will go and do it.” He may ask what a passing score looks like, or whether you think the company will make it. He has spent $210,000 and he employs 147 people, and he will mention both.

You must decline, and decline without lecturing her. A team that answers the question has failed the exercise regardless of how good their determinations are — and that failure is worth more to them than the finding.

5

The overnight evidence request

You have one written request and limited time. Everything you fail to ask for, you will not have.

  • Name artifacts, not topics. Day 3 applies here exactly.
  • Say which objective each item serves — it forces you to check that you need it.
  • Name the custodian. At Meridian this is often the same person, which is itself worth noting.
  • Ask for what would disconfirm the OSC's position, not only what would confirm it.
Read what comes back, not what you hoped would come back

Some of the supplemental material will be responsive. Some will not be. Some of what you asked for will not exist. For every item you requested, record one of three outcomes: received and responsive, received and not responsive, or not provided.

A partly responsive answer is the normal case in a real assessment, and it is where assessments quietly fail. A document arrives, it is roughly on the subject, the schedule is tight, and it gets accepted. The third category is a finding in its own right.

6

Determinations, findings, and the out-brief

A determination is MET, NOT MET, or NOT APPLICABLE at the objective level. A finding is what you write about a NOT MET: which objective failed, what evidence you examined, and what was missing. Severity follows from the requirement and the exposure, not from how annoyed you are about it.

Rule on both incidents in Meridian's history — the October 2023 spearphishing that CrowdStrike blocked, and the February 2024 lost unencrypted USB drive — and on anything the interviews surface. One of the two triggers the DFARS 252.204-7012 obligation and one is genuinely arguable. If you rule the same way on both without distinguishing them, read them again. Note also that the company filed nothing on either, and that the non-reporting decisions were made informally with no documented analysis against the clause.

Quality assurance before the out-brief

The CAP requires assessment results to be compiled and quality-reviewed before the out-brief, and requires the quality reviewer to be a CCA who is not on the assessment team and who has had no contact with it during the assessment.

Deliver the results. Do not deliver advice. Expect at least one challenge, and be ready to concede — a team that never concedes anything has stopped assessing and started defending.

What the report is really for

Whether a stranger could act on it. A contracting officer, an appeals panel, or the next assessment team should be able to read it and know exactly what you looked at, what you concluded, and why — without you in the room to explain.

Meridian — your turn

Meridian · Day 5 Morning · Deliverable D8

Run the assessment, write the findings

The case workbench holds the full evidence package, the interview personas, the incident records, and the findings workspace. Work the two-day sequence in one session:

  • In-brief — cover the CAP minimum. Nominate a minute-taker first.
  • Evidence review — nine documents, logged as you go. Do not leave determinations to memory.
  • Checkpoint — brief preliminary findings. Expect pushback on at least one call.
  • Evidence request — one page, artifacts not topics, with the objective and custodian named.
  • Interviews — choose who, and be able to say why. Open questions only.
  • Determinations and incident rulings — objective level, evidence cited.
  • Out-brief — results, not advice. Concede where the evidence moves you.

Then write the Assessment Findings Report: scope statement, methodology including sampling values, determinations with evidence cited, findings with severity, incident analysis with clause reasoning, and a preliminary score with the outcome you would recommend.

Every finding traceable to evidence you actually examined. If you did not see it, you cannot cite it. Where evidence was requested and not provided, say so — absence of evidence is a result and belongs in the report. And no remediation advice anywhere, including in the recommendation section.
