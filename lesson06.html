Day 4 · Morning · CAP Phase 2 · CCP 35% / CCA 40%

Assessing the Fourteen Domains

Everything you have built — the boundary, the evidence discipline, the objective-level thinking — gets pointed at fourteen families of requirements and turned into determinations. Every small contractor fails in roughly the same places. Knowing the pattern tells you where to look first. It does not tell you what you will find.

Day 4 — AM ~3 hours Highest exam weight Tool: Domain Worksheet

Regulatory status as of 16 November 2026: Phase 2 is suspended; check again before you teach.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to…

  • Assess requirements across the fourteen NIST SP 800-171 Rev 2 families using the CMMC Assessment Guide for Level 2. Bloom: Evaluate
  • Render and justify MET, NOT MET, and NOT APPLICABLE determinations at the assessment-objective level. Bloom: Evaluate
  • Recognize the characteristic failure pattern of each domain in a small-contractor environment. Bloom: Remember
  • Defend a determination against challenge, arguing from the objectives rather than from experience or seniority. Bloom: Evaluate
  • Detect where a gap has been mapped to the wrong requirement, and argue the correct mapping. Bloom: Analyze

1

The three determinations

DeterminationWhat it meansWhat it requires of you
METAll applicable assessment objectives are satisfied by final-form evidence. An enduring exception with a documented mitigation, or a temporary deficiency covered by an operational plan of action, can still be MET.Cite the evidence for each objective. “The control is in place” is not a determination.
NOT METOne or more objectives is not satisfied.Document the nonconformance — which objective, and what is missing. A NOT MET with no named objective cannot be defended or remediated.
NOT APPLICABLEThe requirement does not apply to the assessment scope.Justify it. N/A is treated as MET for scoring purposes, which is exactly why it attracts pressure.
Exam tip

Candidates reliably miss that a requirement can be MET while a deficiency exists, provided the deficiency is temporary and covered by an operational plan of action documented in the SSP — or is an enduring exception with a documented mitigation. Note that this is a different instrument from the POA&M that supports conditional status, which you meet on Friday.

The fourteen domains and how they fail

AC — Access ControlShared accounts, administrative rights used for daily work, no access matrix, accounts that outlive their owners.
AT — Awareness and TrainingA generic annual campaign with partial completion, and no role-based training for the people who actually handle CUI.
AU — Audit and AccountabilityLogging enabled and never reviewed. Retention undefined. No correlation across sources.
CA — Security AssessmentNo SSP, or an SSP written once and never maintained. No cadence for reassessment.
CM — Configuration ManagementNo baseline configurations, no authorized software list, changes made on request without review.
IA — Identification and AuthenticationMulti-factor on the easy system and nothing else. Legacy systems exempted by silence.
IR — Incident ResponseNo written plan, incidents handled by email, and a reporting decision made by whoever happened to be in the room.
MA — MaintenanceThird-party maintenance with standing remote access, no escort, no session records, no sanitization requirement.
MP — Media ProtectionNo removable media policy, no sanitization record, and paper nobody thought of as media.
PE — Physical ProtectionVisitor logs unmaintained, secondary sites uncontrolled, physical access to the server room undocumented.

Read that table against Meridian and you have most of the gap list before anyone opens a document. That is useful for knowing where to look. It is not a substitute for looking.

3

Working a requirement objective by objective

Take AC.L2-3.1.5 — employ the principle of least privilege, and work it against Meridian as the company stood before its remediation year.

ObjectiveEvidence availableDetermination
Privileged accounts are identifiedNo access control matrix exists; the IT Manager manages permissions from memoryNOT MET
Access to privileged accounts is authorized in accordance with least privilegeTwo domain administrators, both using those accounts for daily work; three shared service accounts, one of them domain adminNOT MET
Security functions are identifiedNot documented anywhereNOT MET
Access to security functions is authorized in accordance with least privilegeSameNOT MET

Requirement determination: NOT MET, and the finding names all four objectives rather than asserting “least privilege is not implemented.” The difference matters when the OSC challenges it: a finding that names objectives can be verified or overturned without redoing the assessment.

Citing evidence

“Interview with the IT Manager, 14 March, on privileged account inventory” is a citation. “Discussion” is not. Every MET must name the evidence relied on; every NOT MET must name the objective that fails and what was missing.

4

When the gap list maps to the wrong requirement

Meridian's own documentation maps thirteen gaps to requirements. Two of those mappings are worth arguing with, and noticing them is a specific assessable skill.

GapCompany mappingThe argument
GAP-04 — No asset inventory; unpatched engineering workstations3.4.2, described by the company as “component inventory” The company's description of 3.4.2 is wrong. In Rev 2, 3.4.1 covers baseline configurations and inventories; 3.4.2 is establishing and enforcing security configuration settings. This is really two gaps against two requirements. The correction does not move the score — both are 5 points — which is exactly why it is worth catching: it changes what evidence closes it.
GAP-05 — No formal POA&M; informal spreadsheet, not AO-approved3.12.4 3.12.4 is the System Security Plan requirement. A deficient plan of action is CA.L2-3.12.2. The consequence is not academic: 3.12.4 is one of the six that may never be placed on a POA&M and 3.12.2 is not, so the mapping helps decide whether Meridian is blocked outright.
GAP-08 — No segmentation between CORP-LAN and ENG-LAN3.13.3 3.13.3 separates user functionality from system management functionality — arguably engaged by the policy-only MGMT-LAN separation, but not what the finding describes. What is described is failure to protect communications at a key internal boundary: SC.L2-3.13.1, worth 5 points rather than 1.
GAP-06 — No supply chain risk management programme3.17.1 / 3.17.2 These practices do not exist in Rev 2. The SR family belongs to NIST SP 800-171 Rev 3. CMMC Level 2 is anchored to Rev 2, which has fourteen families and no SR family. There is nothing to deduct under the methodology — and the gap is real: it is a DFARS 252.204-7012(m) flow-down violation and an ESP failure under 32 CFR §170.19.
Exam tip

Three of these are mapping errors and one is a category error. The GAP-06 line is the one to dwell on: a gap mapped to a requirement that does not exist in the applicable revision cannot be scored, cannot be remediated against a stated objective, and cannot be defended — yet the underlying failure is one of the most serious in the company. Catching it requires knowing which revision governs, which is a fact about the programme rather than about this contractor.

5

N/A discipline

NOT APPLICABLE scores as MET. That makes it the determination an OSC has the strongest incentive to invite, and the one you should be slowest to grant.

Meridian's own SPRS submission claimed three:

  • 3.13.3 — “Not applicable, single site network.” The company has two sites, in different states, on one flat LAN. The premise is false on the company's own facts.
  • 3.2.2 — “Not applicable, training is delivered by a third party.” Who delivers training has no bearing on whether the requirement applies. Outsourcing execution does not outsource the obligation.
  • 3.10.3 — “Not applicable, no visitors access the server room.” An assertion by the party being assessed, contradicted by a warehouse with no visitor log where delivery drivers come and go.
Recognize when N/A is being invited

None of these three is a considered determination. Each is a reason not to do work, written by someone who needed a number. An assessor who accepts them has allowed the OSC to score itself.

6

Defending a determination

Challenges are arbitrated against the Assessment Guide text, not by assertion and not by seniority. If a determination cannot be defended from the objectives, it changes.

This cuts both ways, and the second direction is the one people find harder. A team that never concedes anything has stopped assessing and started defending. Distinguish new information, which should change your call, from pressure, which should not.

Lead with the call you are least sure of

The instinct is to present your best work. That wastes the exercise: nobody learns anything from a determination everyone agrees with. A categorization or determination that cannot survive a peer challenge will not survive an organization that stands to save money by defeating it.

7

The Meridian case

Meridian · Day 4 Morning · Deliverable D6 · Fictional case

Render objective-level determinations

The Domain Determination Worksheet loads fifteen requirements spanning eight of the fourteen families, decomposed into their assessment objectives, with the Meridian evidence attached to each.

For every requirement: determine each objective, cite the evidence relied on, and give the reasoning where the call was not obvious. Where you dispute the company's mapping of a gap to a requirement, say so and give your mapping — the tool checks both G-08 and G-09 specifically.

  • Objective level, not requirement level. A worksheet that determines at the requirement level has not done the work.
  • Every MET cites named evidence. “Interview with the IT Manager” is a citation; “discussion” is not.
  • Every NOT MET names the failing objective and what is missing.
  • Every N/A carries a justification. N/A scores as MET, so it will be challenged.
  • No remediation. State what is missing; do not state what to do about it.

Your determinations must stay consistent with the scope you drew this morning and the evidence you triaged yesterday. The tool flags contradictions.

For faculty

Faculty teaching segment

  • Signature assignment: Students render objective-level determinations for a set of requirements against fictional evidence (the D6 deliverable), citing evidence for every MET and stating every NOT MET gap without a fix.
  • Rubric criteria:
    • Determinations are made at the objective level, not the requirement level
    • Every MET cites named evidence (an interview with a named role, a specific artifact)
    • Every NOT MET states the gap precisely without recommending a fix
    • Disputed company mappings are challenged with the student’s own mapping
  • Lab idea: Teams split the Domain Determination Worksheet’s fifteen requirements and cross-present.
  • Course fit: Security controls (NIST SP 800-171), IT audit, compliance.
🎓 Teaching noteRequire teams to lead with their least comfortable determination. Nobody learns from one everyone agrees with.

CMMC Practice · A7⏱ 40 min

Objective-Level Determinations

Render MET, NOT MET, or N/A at the objective level, with evidence.

Assessment Lab · B6⏱ 75 min

Domain Determination Worksheet

Determine fifteen requirements across eight families against Meridian evidence.

Knowledge Check

Test your judgment

Ten questions in CCP style. Read every rationale.

Q1.Meridian's SPRS submission marks 3.2.2 "Not applicable — training is delivered by a third party." What is the assessor's response?

Question 1 options
Correct: A. Outsourcing execution does not outsource the obligation. This is not a determination; it is a reason not to do work, written by someone who needed a number. Why the others fall short: B That would be a MET determination on the provider's evidence, not an N/A. And no such evidence was offered.; C A training vendor's certification, if it existed, would say nothing about whether Meridian's people received role-based training.; D There is no provider assessment, and the requirement is Meridian's.

Q2.Meridian maps "No System Security Plan exists" to requirement 3.12.1. What is the strongest correction?

Question 2 options
Correct: B. Two consequences follow the correction: the deduction falls from 5 to 1, and the requirement becomes an absolute barrier to any CMMC status regardless of score. The cheapest requirement on the sheet is the one that stops the company. Why the others fall short: A 3.12.1 is periodic assessment of security controls. It is a different obligation.; C Defensible as an argument, and it raises total deductions rather than lowering them. The cleaner correction is 3.12.4, which is the requirement that actually addresses the SSP.; D Monitoring is a separate obligation and does not describe the absence of an SSP.

Q3.Which statement about NOT APPLICABLE is correct?

Question 3 options
Correct: C. Which is precisely why it attracts pressure from an OSC and why an assessor should be slow to grant it. Why the others fall short: A N/A is not a failure. It means the requirement does not apply to the scope.; B SSP documentation supports the justification, and the determination is the assessor's regardless of what the SSP says.; D It is available and used. What is unavailable at Level 2 is partial credit at the objective level.

Q4.An assessor writes: "AC.L2-3.1.5 NOT MET — least privilege is not implemented." What is deficient about this finding?

Question 4 options
Correct: D. A NOT MET with no named objective cannot be defended or remediated. The finding should name each objective that failed, the evidence examined, and what was absent. Why the others fall short: A It states a conclusion. A reader cannot verify or overturn it without redoing the assessment.; B It should not. Naming the fix is consulting, and it is still consulting in a findings report.; C Severity follows from the requirement and the exposure, and it does not cure the absence of objective-level detail.

Q5.A requirement's evidence shows a temporary deficiency that the OSC has documented in its SSP with an operational plan of action. Can the requirement be MET?

Question 5 options
Correct: C. Note that this instrument is distinct from the POA&M supporting conditional status — the two are routinely conflated, and they operate at different points in the process. Why the others fall short: A Too absolute. The Assessment Guide contemplates both enduring exceptions with documented mitigations and temporary deficiencies covered by operational plans of action.; B Point values belong to the DoD Assessment score and have nothing to do with the MET determination.; D No such concurrence is contemplated in the determination process.

Q6.Meridian maps its unsegmented CORP-LAN / ENG-LAN routing to 3.13.3. What is the better mapping?

Question 6 options
Correct: A. What is described is a failure of boundary protection at a key internal boundary. It is also worth 5 points rather than 1, so the correction raises the deduction. Why the others fall short: B That addresses separating publicly accessible components, which is not what is described.; C 3.13.3 separates user functionality from system management functionality. That is a different idea from segmenting untrusted endpoints from CUI servers.; D Arguable, and it addresses information flow enforcement rather than boundary protection. 3.13.1 is the cleaner call on these facts.

Q7.During a cross-presentation an opposing team challenges your determination and produces evidence you had not seen. What should happen?

Question 7 options
Correct: D. Distinguish new information, which should change your call, from pressure, which should not. Conceding to evidence is the discipline; conceding to insistence is the failure. Why the others fall short: A A team that never concedes anything has stopped assessing and started defending.; B Arbitration happens against the Assessment Guide text, and here there is nothing to arbitrate — new evidence has been produced.; C A report carries a determination, not a disagreement between assessors about what the evidence shows.

Q8.Which is a proper evidence citation supporting a MET determination?

Question 8 options
Correct: B. Names the method, the individual, the date, the subject, and the corroborating artifact. A reader could go and check it. Why the others fall short: A Names neither the person, the date, nor the subject. It is not a citation.; C "It is in the SSP" is the belief that having a document means the control is implemented. The SSP is a claim about the environment, not evidence of it.; D No object, no date, no detail of what was observed. It cannot be verified or overturned.

Q9.Meridian claims 3.10.3 (escort visitors) is N/A because "no visitors access the server room." What defeats this?

Question 9 options
Correct: D. The claim also confines itself to the server room, while the requirement concerns the organization's facilities within the scope. And an unlogged site cannot evidence who did or did not enter. Why the others fall short: A The premise is an assertion by the party being assessed, and it is contradicted elsewhere in the environment.; B Overstated. What is true is that 3.10.3 is on the list that may never be placed on a POA&M, which is a different rule.; C No size exemption exists anywhere in the model.

Q10.Why does knowing each domain's characteristic small-contractor failure pattern help an assessor?

Question 10 options
Correct: C. Useful for planning evidence requests and interview questions. It becomes dangerous the moment it substitutes for looking. Why the others fall short: A The opposite of what an assessor does. A pattern is a hypothesis, not a determination.; B Severity follows from the requirement and the exposure in the specific environment.; D Scoping follows the asset categories, not the likelihood of failure.
