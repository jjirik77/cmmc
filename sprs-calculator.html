The weighting

Two partial-credit exceptions, and only two

3.5.3 — MFA. Deduct 3 where MFA is implemented for remote and privileged users but not the general user. Deduct 5 where MFA is not implemented for any users.

3.13.11 — FIPS-validated encryption. Deduct 3 where encryption is employed but not FIPS validated. Deduct 5 where encryption is not employed.

Every other requirement is all or nothing. Awarding partial credit elsewhere because an organization has “mostly” done something is the most common scoring error there is.

Maximum 110. Floor −203 — the weighted deductions total far more than 110, so a negative score is not a computational error and contractors do post them. Look each value up in the methodology; do not work from memory.

On the point values

Every value above comes from the NIST SP 800-171 DoD Assessment Methodology, Section 5 and Annex A. Look them up yourself rather than working from this table or from memory — the discipline is the skill, and the methodology is the authority.

Defend three deductions

At least one must be a deduction where you believe the company has mapped the gap to the wrong requirement. State the mapping you would use and why.

Model answer

The two mappings worth disputing, and what each does to the total:

CorrectionEffect on the score
GAP-04 split into 3.4.1 (inventory) and 3.4.2 (configuration settings)Deductions rise to 56; score falls to 54
GAP-08 to 3.13.1 (5 points instead of 1)Deductions rise to 55; score falls to 55
Both corrections togetherDeductions 60; score 50
GAP-05 to 3.12.2 instead of 3.12.4Changes the eligibility analysis rather than the arithmetic — see below

Any figure in the range 50 to 59, with the reasoning shown, is a correct answer. A bare number with no working is not.

The most consequential correction barely moves the score

Re-mapping the POA&M deficiency from 3.12.4 to 3.12.2 changes almost nothing arithmetically. It changes whether one of the six never-defer requirements is failing — which decides whether any CMMC status can issue at all, at any score.

Scoring weight measures exploitability. POA&M eligibility measures something else entirely. A student who notices that the most important correction on the sheet is nearly invisible in the total has understood the difference between the two instruments.

Also worth defending: G-01 at 5 rather than 3 (the conjunction, not a spectrum); G-03b at 3 rather than 5 (not all AU requirements are 5); G-06b at 1 rather than 5 (not in either enumerated list); and G-13 at 0 (not a NIST requirement at all).

3

What your score does and does not represent

Three or four sentences. Be precise about what a gap list is, and about the difference between this figure and a CMMC certification determination.

Model answer

Three things a strong answer says:

  • 59 is an optimistic ceiling, not a floor. Eight seeded findings expanded across fourteen lines is a gap analysis of selected areas, not an assessment of 110 requirements. A real assessment examines all of them and would find more NOT MET — media disposal, physical access at Dayton, and the uncontrolled data flow into AWS GovCloud are three this list never reaches. The true score is at most 59.
  • The score is not the only gate. At least one and arguably two of the six requirements that may never be placed on a POA&M are failing — PE.L2-3.10.3, and CA.L2-3.12.4 if the SSP’s own “needs update” status is taken at face value. Even above 88, those must be MET outright before any status could issue.
  • This is a different instrument from a certification determination. The DoD Assessment Methodology is a weighted strategic risk score. A CMMC determination is binary at the objective level with no partial credit. Conflating them is the error the session exists to prevent.
4

INC-003 — the incident decision

Wednesday, 09:40

Trevor Nakamura, reconciling VPN accounts against the HR separation list in preparation for the assessment, finds that one of the six stale accounts — jhollis, belonging to a design engineer who left in November — shows successful VPN authentications on four dates over the past six weeks, the most recent eleven days ago. Each session lasted between twenty and ninety minutes.

What is known: the account was never disabled. It held membership in the engineering security group, which grants read and write access to the CUI tree on SYS-003 and to the Arena PLM. The VPN has no MFA. The account's password was last set fourteen months ago and has not changed.

What is not known: who authenticated. Whether anything was accessed, opened, or copied. Whether the sessions originated from the former employee, from someone using credentials he left behind, or from an unrelated party. The FortiGate retains authentication events; SYS-003 and SYS-004 retain 30 days of logs and are not reviewed, so file-level access records for three of the four sessions have already rolled off.

Nakamura's position: “It is almost certainly Jared. He was finishing a handover and he is a good guy. Nothing has been taken — we would see it.”

Model answer

Does it trigger?Yes. A cyber incident includes actions resulting in an actual or potentially adverse effect on an information system or the information residing in it. Unauthorised authentication to a network holding CUI, by an account that should not exist, with write access to the CUI store, is not a potential compromise — it is an actual unauthorised access. The only open question is what was done with it.
Which paragraph252.204-7012(c)(1) for the rapid report; the definition of cyber incident at (a) supplies the threshold. Media preservation under (e) attaches once reported — which matters urgently here, because logs are actively rolling off.
Clock start72 hours from discovery — Wednesday 09:40, when Nakamura identified the authentications. Not from the first session six weeks ago, and not from the separation date. Document the six-week gap: a contracting officer will ask how an account authenticated four times without anyone noticing, and the answer is evidence about the monitoring capability rather than about the clock.
Who decidesNot Nakamura. Meridian has an IRP naming an Incident Commander, and that plan is three years stale and lists a departed employee — so in practice the CISO and the CEO, who is also the Authorizing Official, must be involved. That the decision has nowhere to go is itself a finding under IR.L2-3.6.1.
What preservation requires nowImages of affected systems and relevant monitoring data for at least 90 days from the report. With 30-day retention and no review, the preservation obligation is already partly impossible to satisfy for the earlier sessions. Preserving what remains is urgent and is the first thing an assessor would ask whether anyone did.

The counter-argument: it is almost certainly the former employee finishing a handover; no evidence of exfiltration; reporting an incident involving a trusted ex-colleague is disproportionate.

The answer: the threshold is “potentially adverse,” not “malicious,” and reporting is not an accusation. An authorised handover would have been performed on an authorised account. “Almost certainly Jared” is an inference about identity that the environment cannot support: with no MFA, the account proves possession of a credential and nothing whatever about who held it.

And Nakamura’s second claim — “nothing has been taken, we would see it” — is an appeal to the absence of evidence. Ask whether Meridian has the capability to have observed what he says has not happened. It does not: no file-access review, no SIEM, no correlation, and 30-day retention that has already consumed most of the record.

Could this organization know what it claims to know? That is the question you will ask for the rest of your career.
