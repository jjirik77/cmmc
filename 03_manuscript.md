# Lesson 0 — CMMC Orientation and the Assessor Mindset
*Day 1 &middot; Morning &middot; Orientation*

CMMC is not a cybersecurity test. It is a conformity regime, and you are training to be the person whose determination other people rely on without knowing you. Before the first requirement, understand what the program is, who governs which part of it, what the credential pathway actually requires, and the change of role that everything else in this week depends on.

## Learning objectives
By the end of this session you will be able to:

- **[Explain]** State what CMMC is, what it governs, and what makes it different in kind from the frameworks you already know.
- **[Identify]** Name every body in the CMMC ecosystem and say which one governs organizations and which governs individuals.
- **[Distinguish]** Separate the assessor role from the consultant role, and name the moments where the line is easiest to cross.
- **[Apply]** Apply the three-part standard &mdash; requirement, authority, evidence &mdash; to a claim about a contractor.
- **[Plan]** Map the CCP and CCA prerequisite chains against your own position today.

## Key terminology

- **CMMC** — Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification. The DoD program, established by 32 CFR Part 170, that makes a stated level of cybersecurity a condition of contract award.
- **OSC** — Organization Seeking Certification — the contractor being assessed. The term of art in 32 CFR Part 170; use it rather than “the client.”
- **C3PAO** — CMMC Third-Party Assessment Organization. An accredited firm authorized to conduct Level 2 certification assessments.
- **CAICO** — CMMC Assessor and Instructor Certification Organization. ISACA holds this role since December 2025; the transition completed April 2026.
- **CCP / CCA** — Certified CMMC Professional and Certified CMMC Assessor. Individual credentials issued by ISACA, not by the Cyber AB.
- **Affirming Official** — The senior official of the OSC with authority to bind it, who submits the annual affirmation in SPRS. Personally exposed under the False Claims Act.
- **SPRS** — Supplier Performance Risk System. Where a DoD Assessment score is posted and where the annual affirmation is filed.
- **CUI** — Controlled Unclassified Information. Information a law, regulation, or government-wide policy requires an agency to safeguard. Level 2 exists to protect it.
- **FCI** — Federal Contract Information. Non-public information provided by or generated for the Government under a contract. Triggers Level 1 and the 15 FAR requirements.
- **CAP** — CMMC Assessment Process v2.0. The Cyber AB document that governs how an assessment is run: preliminary proceedings plus four numbered phases.
- **CoPC** — CMMC Code of Professional Conduct v2.0. Eight guiding principles that govern assessor behaviour, including impartiality and the three-year consulting bar.
- **Assessment objective** — The unit of determination. The 110 Level 2 requirements decompose into roughly 320 objectives, each of which must be MET or NOT APPLICABLE.

## 1. What this workshop is, and what it is not

Five days, ten sessions, one company. You will scope it, plan the engagement, request and triage its evidence,
render determinations against the fourteen NIST SP 800-171 Rev 2 families, compute and defend its DoD Assessment
score, run a two-day mock assessment against it, write the findings, and close it out with a plan of action.
On the final afternoon you will be handed a company you have never seen and given thirty minutes.

This is preparation for the ISACA CCP and CCA credentials, and it is
preparation for the work those credentials describe. Those are not the same thing, and where they diverge this
workshop follows the work. An exam-only course teaches you the model. This one teaches you to render a
determination you can defend when the company that dislikes it pushes back.

What it is not

- Not a course in how to become compliant. You will spend five days finding problems in a
company and never once being permitted to fix one. That constraint is the subject, not an inconvenience.

- Not a technical security course. You are expected to know what a domain controller and a
Conditional Access policy are. You are not expected to configure either.

- Not a substitute for the primary sources. Everything here is traced to 32 CFR Part 170, the
FAR, the DFARS, the NIST publications, the CAP, and the NARA CUI Registry. When this material and a governing
document disagree, the governing document wins and this material is wrong.

Exam tip

The single most common source of wrong answers in CMMC study is confidently remembered material that was true
two years ago. Two examples arrive in this workshop within the first day: who issues the CCP and CCA, and how many
requirements CMMC Level 1 comprises. Both are widely mis-stated in circulating training decks. Cite the rule, not
the deck.

## 2. Where CMMC sits among the frameworks you know

Most people arriving at CMMC already know a security framework, and the instinct is to file CMMC alongside it.
That instinct produces one specific, predictable error later: the assumption that partial implementation earns
partial credit. True in a maturity model. False in CMMC.

The differences that matter are not about content. CMMC Level 2 is NIST SP 800-171 Rev 2 — the
requirements are borrowed wholesale. What differs is status, enforcement, and how conformity is demonstrated.

auditor of a voluntary framework does not.

Exam tip — the counting trap

Level 1 is 15 requirements. A great deal of circulating CMMC 2.0 material says 17, because
earlier model documentation split FAR 52.204-21(b)(1)(ix) into three separate practices. That split has no basis in
the rule. 32 CFR 170.14(c)(2) incorporates fifteen paragraphs, and there are fifteen of them. Count them yourself.

Level 2 decomposes further, and this is the mechanic that decides most of your week. The 110 requirements break
into roughly 320 assessment objectives in NIST SP 800-171A. Every objective under a requirement
must be MET or NOT APPLICABLE for the parent requirement to be MET. There is no partial credit at the objective
level, and an assessor who determines at the requirement level has not done the work.

## 5. The shift: consultant to assessor

An assessor is not a better consultant. The two roles answer different questions, serve different parties, and
are held to different standards. Most students find this the hardest idea in the workshop, and it is not because
it is complicated. It is because the rules prohibit things people are used to doing: helping, advising, fixing,
recommending a vendor they trust.

The last row is the one that changes your working life. As an assessor you operate inside a conformity regime

The line, in one sentence

A consultant is paid to make an organization better. An assessor is paid to say accurately what is true about
it. You will feel the pull constantly — a company with obvious problems, staff who want help, an easy fix you
could name in one sentence. Noticing the pull is fine. Acting on it disqualifies you.

## 6. The three-part standard

From this session onward, every claim you make in this workshop is held to three questions. Write them on
something you will still have on Friday.

The questionWhat a weak answer looks like

RequirementWhat specifically is required?
“They need to protect CUI.” Which requirement, and at which objective?

AuthorityWhat imposes it?
“CMMC requires it.” CMMC is a program. Name the clause, the section, or the Registry category.

EvidenceWhat would demonstrate it, and would that be enough?
“They have a policy.” A policy is a claim about intent, not proof of practice.

Days 1 and 2 exercise the first two properly and the third lightly. Day 3 afternoon onward is almost entirely
the third, and it is the part that separates people who know CMMC from people who can assess against it.

## 8. Responsible use, and what never leaves the boundary

The CoPC's eighth guiding principle is Proper Use of Technology and AI, and it is not
decorative. It exists because two things have become easy at the same time: pasting a client's system security
plan into a chat window, and letting a tool draft a determination that is yours to make.

Two questions, every time

- Is a tool making a determination that is mine to make? A model can summarize an artifact. It
cannot render MET or NOT MET, because it cannot be held to the CoPC, cannot be challenged at an out-brief, and
cannot sign anything. The determination is the credentialed human's, and the reasoning has to be theirs.

- Is customer data leaving the boundary to reach that tool? An SSP extract, a Conditional Access
export, a network diagram, an access control matrix, personnel names, CAGE codes, incident detail — all of it
is confidential to the engagement and much of it describes exactly how to attack the OSC. Confidentiality is a
separate CoPC principle and it does not have a convenience exception.

A real scenario, adjudicated on Day 2

To save time on a long evidence review, a team member pastes several paragraphs of the OSC's system security
plan and a screenshot of its access-control configuration into a public web-based AI assistant, and asks it to
draft finding language. They read the output, edit it, and enter it as their determination.

Two principles are engaged, not one, and the disposition is not “be careful next time.” You will rule
on this scenario, cite the practices, and say who decides.

What this workshop asks of you

- Nothing from the Meridian case needs protecting — it is fictional, and you may work it in any tool you
like. Use the week to build the habit anyway, because the habit is what transfers.

- Every claim you write carries its authority. If an AI tool supplied a citation, open the citation. Fabricated
regulatory cites are common, confident, and career-ending in a findings report.

- Disclose tool use where an engagement requires it, and never let a tool talk to the OSC.

## Knowledge check

**Q1.** Which body certifies individuals as CCPs and CCAs?
  - A. The Cyber AB
    - *Feedback:* The Cyber AB is the accreditation body. It accredits C3PAOs under ISO/IEC 17020 and operates the Marketplace — organizations, not people. This was true historically, which is why so much circulating material still says it.
  - B. ISACA, acting as the CAICO **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* ISACA took the CMMC Assessor and Instructor Certification Organization role in December 2025, and the transition completed in April 2026. It administers CCP, CCA, Lead CCA, and CCI.
  - C. The DoW CIO / CMMC PMO
    - *Feedback:* The PMO owns the program and issues the model, assessment guides, and scoping guides. It does not certify individuals.
  - D. DIBCAC
    - *Feedback:* DIBCAC is the government assessment arm and conducts government-led assessments, including Level 3. It has no credentialing role.

**Q2.** A contractor holds Federal Contract Information but no CUI. What CMMC level applies?
  - A. Level 1 **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* FCI triggers FAR 52.204-21 and CMMC Level 1 — 15 requirements, annual self-assessment and affirmation.
  - B. Level 2, at reduced scope
    - *Feedback:* There is no reduced-scope Level 2. Level 2 is triggered by CUI, and its requirement set is not scaled down by how little CUI is present.
  - C. Level 2
    - *Feedback:* Level 2 is triggered by CUI. FCI alone does not reach it.
  - D. None — FCI is outside CMMC
    - *Feedback:* A common and expensive misreading. FCI is squarely inside CMMC at Level 1; 32 CFR 170.14(c)(2) incorporates the fifteen FAR safeguarding requirements.

**Q3.** How many security requirements comprise CMMC Level 1?
  - A. 15 **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* 32 CFR 170.14(c)(2) incorporates FAR 52.204-21(b)(1)(i) through (xv). Fifteen paragraphs, fifteen requirements.
  - B. 17
    - *Feedback:* The most widely repeated wrong answer in CMMC study. Earlier CMMC 2.0 model documentation split FAR 52.204-21(b)(1)(ix) into three practices. The split has no basis in the rule. If a deck says 17, the deck is out of date.
  - C. 110
    - *Feedback:* 110 is Level 2, from NIST SP 800-171 Rev 2.
  - D. 320
    - *Feedback:* Roughly 320 is the count of Level 2 assessment objectives in NIST SP 800-171A, not requirements, and not Level 1.

**Q4.** Under Level 2, a requirement has four assessment objectives. Three are MET and one is not. What is the determination for the requirement?
  - A. MET, with a noted deficiency
    - *Feedback:* There is no such determination. A noted deficiency at objective level is exactly what makes the requirement NOT MET.
  - B. Partially MET
    - *Feedback:* Partially MET does not exist in CMMC. This is the single most consequential import from maturity-model thinking, and it produces determinations that cannot be defended.
  - C. NOT MET **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* Every assessment objective must be MET or NOT APPLICABLE for the parent requirement to be MET. One objective unmet makes the whole requirement NOT MET. There is no partial credit.
  - D. It depends on the point value of the requirement
    - *Feedback:* Point values belong to the DoD Assessment Methodology score, which is a different instrument. They have nothing to do with the MET / NOT MET determination.

**Q5.** An assessor reviewing evidence notices an obvious, cheap fix for a gap and mentions it to the IT Manager during the walkthrough. What has happened?
  - A. Nothing — informal observations are not part of the report
    - *Feedback:* The prohibition is not about the report. It is about the role. Advice given verbally in a corridor is still advice, and it is still consulting.
  - B. Good practice — the assessor is adding value
    - *Feedback:* This is the reasoning that ends careers, and it feels helpful at the time. Adding value to the OSC is the consultant's job, and doing it inside an assessment destroys the independence the determination rests on.
  - C. The assessor has crossed from assessment into consulting **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* An assessor records a problem, evidences it, and says nothing about the remedy. Naming a risk is assessment; naming its fix is not.
  - D. It is permitted if the OSC asked first
    - *Feedback:* The OSC will ask. It will ask reasonably, warmly, and repeatedly. Being asked does not change the rule — and being able to decline without lecturing is a skill this workshop assesses.

**Q6.** Which of these is the correct three-part standard for a claim in an assessment finding?
  - A. Risk, impact, likelihood
    - *Feedback:* That is a risk-assessment triad. It is not how a conformity determination is justified.
  - B. Requirement, authority, evidence **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* What specifically is required; what regulation, clause, or official document imposes it; and what would demonstrate it — and whether that would be enough.
  - C. Policy, procedure, practice
    - *Feedback:* A useful maturity heuristic, and not the standard for defending a determination. It names artifact types, not the burden of proof.
  - D. Examine, interview, test
    - *Feedback:* Those are the three assessment methods from NIST SP 800-171A. They are how you gather evidence, not how you justify a claim.

**Q7.** Who may serve as an OSC's Affirming Official?
  - A. The IT Manager who knows the environment best
    - *Feedback:* Knowledge of the environment is not the criterion. Authority to bind the organization is.
  - B. The C3PAO's Lead CCA
    - *Feedback:* Never. The assessor does not affirm the organization's compliance; that would collapse the independence the whole regime rests on.
  - C. A senior official of the OSC with authority to bind it **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* And the exposure attaches personally: an affirmation that is not true is a false statement in a federal system, with False Claims Act consequences for the person who signed it.
  - D. The consultant who prepared the organization
    - *Feedback:* A consultant cannot bind the OSC and cannot affirm on its behalf.

**Q8.** An assessment team member pastes extracts from the OSC's system security plan into a public AI assistant to draft finding language. Which CoPC principles are engaged?
  - A. Proper Use of Technology and AI only
    - *Feedback:* It is the obvious one, and it is not the only one. Stopping here misses the more serious breach.
  - B. Confidentiality only
    - *Feedback:* Confidentiality is engaged, but so is the question of who is permitted to render the determination.
  - C. Proper Use of Technology and AI, and Confidentiality **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* Two breaches, not one: engagement-confidential information left the boundary, and a tool was used to produce reasoning that is the credentialed assessor's to produce. Information Integrity is arguably a third.
  - D. None, provided the output was edited before use
    - *Feedback:* Editing the output addresses neither problem. The data has already left, and the reasoning still did not originate with the assessor.

**Q9.** What is the relationship between CMMC Level 2 and NIST SP 800-171 Rev 2?
  - A. Level 2 is a subset of the 800-171 requirements chosen by the PMO
    - *Feedback:* There is no subset at Level 2. All 110 apply.
  - B. Level 2 adopts all 110 requirements of Rev 2 as its security requirements **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* The controls are borrowed wholesale. What CMMC adds is the assessment regime, the levels, the scoping rules, affirmation, and enforceability through a contract clause.
  - C. Level 2 is based on Rev 3, with Rev 2 retained for legacy contracts
    - *Feedback:* Rev 3 is not the assessment standard. CMMC Level 2 remains anchored to Rev 2; Rev 3 is horizon-scanning only until future rulemaking says otherwise.
  - D. Level 2 adds requirements from NIST SP 800-172 to 800-171
    - *Feedback:* That describes Level 3, which layers a selected subset of 800-172 on top of the 110.

**Q10.** A Registered Practitioner (RP) helped an OSC prepare for a Level 1 self-assessment two years ago. The RP is now proposed for the OSC's Level 2 certification assessment team. What applies?
  - A. Nothing — the prior work was Level 1, not Level 2
    - *Feedback:* The bar covers preparatory or consulting activity for any type of CMMC assessment. The level of the earlier work does not create an exemption.
  - B. Nothing — two years have passed
    - *Feedback:* The prohibition runs three years, not two. And even outside it, other conflicts may still apply.
  - C. The three-year consulting prohibition bars participation **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* 32 CFR 170.8(b)(17)(ii)(G), carried into CoPC 2.2(d), prohibits participating in the Level 2 certification process where the member consulted to prepare the organization for any CMMC assessment within three years.
  - D. Participation is permitted if the RP discloses it and the OSC consents
    - *Feedback:* The OSC's consent is irrelevant, and the individual does not decide their own eligibility. The C3PAO owns impartiality and conflict management, and that responsibility cannot be delegated.

# Lesson 1 — The Compliance Landscape and the CMMC Ecosystem
*Day 1 &middot; Afternoon &middot; CCP Domains 1 &amp; 3*

Cybersecurity standards do not become obligations on their own. Someone writes one, a government adopts it, and a contracting officer puts it in a contract. Until that happens, a standard is advice. This session is about how CMMC stopped being advice &mdash; and about reading a regulatory disruption against primary sources instead of headlines.

## Learning objectives
By the end of this session you will be able to:

- **[Trace]** Trace the regulatory chain from Executive Order 13556 through 32 CFR Part 170 and the 48 CFR acquisition rule to DFARS 252.204-7021 appearing in a solicitation.
- **[Distinguish]** Distinguish a voluntary framework from a mandatory, assessed, contractually enforced requirement set.
- **[Determine]** Determine the applicable CMMC level from a solicitation that never states one in plain language.
- **[Separate]** Separate what a policy announcement does from what a regulation says, and name the instruments that can actually change the framework.
- **[Analyse]** Sort statements in a professional regulatory alert into what the source states, what the author infers, and what the author advocates.

## Key terminology

- **EO 13556** — Executive Order 13556 (2010). Created the government-wide CUI Program and named NARA as Executive Agent. The first link in the chain.
- **32 CFR Part 2002** — Implements the CUI Program: who may designate CUI, and how it is marked, safeguarded, disseminated, and decontrolled.
- **32 CFR Part 170** — The CMMC Program rule. Effective 16 December 2024. Defines the model, levels, assessment types, scoping, affirmation, POA&M rules, and the ecosystem.
- **48 CFR acquisition rule** — Effective 10 November 2025. Amended the DFARS so contracting officers may insert the CMMC clause into solicitations and contracts. Phase 1 began here.
- **DFARS 252.204-7012** — Safeguarding Covered Defense Information and Cyber Incident Reporting. In force since 2017. Makes 800-171 contractual and adds the 72-hour clock.
- **DFARS 252.204-7019 / -7020** — Require a current DoD Assessment score posted in SPRS as a condition of award.
- **DFARS 252.204-7021** — The CMMC clause. Makes a specific CMMC level and status a condition of award and of continued performance.
- **Flowdown** — The mechanism by which a prime passes safeguarding and certification obligations to subcontractors that will handle covered defense information.
- **Covered defense information** — The term DFARS 252.204-7012 actually uses. Broader than it looks: it captures information the contractor generates during performance, not only what the Government hands over.
- **Class deviation** — A formal acquisition instrument that changes how a clause is applied across a class of contracts. One of the three things that can actually change what a contractor owes.

## 1. Why a cybersecurity standard became a contract term

NIST SP 800-171 was published in 2015 and revised in 2020. On its own it binds nobody. It is a NIST
publication: a specification of 110 security requirements for protecting CUI on nonfederal systems, with no
enforcement mechanism of any kind.

What made it consequential was a clause. DFARS 252.204-7012 took a NIST document and made implementing it a
term of a Department of Defense contract. From that moment a contractor that failed to implement 800-171 was not
falling short of best practice — it was in breach of contract.

CMMC is the layer that came next, and it answers a different question. 7012 said you must implement this.
It did not say and someone will check. Fifteen years of self-attestation produced a defense industrial
base in which the attestations and the reality had drifted a long way apart. CMMC is the verification regime built
on top of an obligation that already existed.

Why this matters for the rest of the week

Hold the two apart. The obligation to protect CUI lives in DFARS 252.204-7012 and dates from
2017. The verification of that obligation lives in 32 CFR Part 170 and DFARS 252.204-7021 and
dates from 2024–2025. The two are separable, and separating them is the single most useful analytical move
available in this field — you will use it again on Friday.

## 4. Reading a solicitation: where the level actually comes from

Solicitation W58RGZ-26-R-0301 — the AMCOM recompete Meridian must win — carries
DFARS 252.204-7021 with the required CMMC status left as a fill-in, and points to Section L. Section L says the
status “shall be that which corresponds to the categories of information identified in Section C, determined
in accordance with 32 CFR Part 170.”

In other words: the solicitation does not tell you. You have to reason it out. The reasoning runs through the
Statement of Work, not the clause list.

What Section C says

- C.3.2 — on award the Government furnishes the complete Technical Data Package: engineering
drawings, pattern grading specifications, seam and stitch construction standards, flame-resistance test protocols,
the approved materials list. Portions bear Distribution Statement D and are subject to export
control provisions cited in Section I.

- C.3.3 — the contractor generates and transmits production status reports, first-article
test results, lot traceability records, and deviation requests, referencing drawing numbers and revision levels
from the TDP.

- C.4.1 — any subcontractor receiving any portion of the TDP must be identified to the
Contracting Officer, and the safeguarding and certification requirements of Section I must be included in that
subcontract.

Technical information with military application, subject to controls on access, use, reproduction, modification,
release, or dissemination, is Controlled Technical Information — a CUI category. CUI drives
Level 2. C.3.3 matters independently: information the contractor generates during performance is captured
too. And C.4.1 is flowdown, stated in the Statement of Work before it appears in any clause.

Exam tip

Candidates look for the level in the clause list and find a blank. The level is determined by the information,
under 32 CFR Part 170, and the Statement of Work is where the information is described. Read Section C before
Section I.

## 5. Policy announcement versus regulation

CMMC is an active programme, and active programmes generate announcements: memoranda, press releases, industry
days, testimony, and a steady stream of professional commentary interpreting all of it. Some of that changes what a
contractor owes. Most of it does not, and telling the two apart is a skill with a longer shelf life than any
particular announcement.

Three instruments can change what is required

- A class deviation — a formal acquisition instrument changing how a clause is applied
across a class of contracts.

- A DFARS or FAR rule change — amending the clause text itself.

- An amendment to 32 CFR Part 170 — changing the programme rule.

Until one of those issues, the framework is what the published instruments say it is. A memorandum announcing an
intention, a schedule adjustment, a pilot, or a review does not amend a regulation, however consequential it may
turn out to be. Neither does a recommendation to an agency official, and neither does a press report about either.

What you are readingDoes it change the requirement?What to do with it

An amendment to 32 CFR Part 170YesRead it; re-derive anything downstream that depended on the old text

A DFARS or FAR rule changeYesRead the new clause text, not the summary of it

A class deviationYes, for the class it namesCheck whether the contract in front of you is in that class

A policy memorandum or announcementNot by itselfNote it; watch for the instrument that would implement it

A task force report or recommendationNoTrack it as a leading indicator, nothing more

A law firm alert or vendor briefingNoUse it to stay current; never cite it

Why an assessor in particular needs this

Because the question arrives as a request. A contractor reads that something has changed, concludes that an
obligation has lifted, and asks you to agree. Your answer is not a matter of opinion or of how the news was
reported — it is whether an instrument issued, and what its text says.

The same discipline protects you in the other direction. An announcement that sounds alarming does not create an
obligation either, and an assessor who writes findings against requirements that were never enacted is as wrong as
one who ignores requirements that were.

Note also that obligations rarely move together. The chain in the previous section has nine links from four
different bodies of law, enacted across fifteen years. A change at one link frequently leaves the others exactly
where they were — which is why “has CMMC changed?” is almost never a question with a single
answer, and why the useful question is always which instrument, and what does its text now say?

## 6. Fact, inference, advice

You are entering a field with a very large amount of published commentary and a very small amount of governing
text. The discipline that keeps you current without making you wrong is the ability to read a professional alert
and sort every sentence into three buckets.

BucketWhat it isExample shapeCitable?

StatedWhat the primary source actually says
“32 CFR 170.21 permits a POA&M only where the score is at least 0.8 of the total.”Yes — cite the source, not the alert

InferredWhat the author concludes from it
“This suggests the Department intends to ease the burden on small suppliers.”No

AdvocatedWhat the author recommends the reader do
“Contractors should begin readiness work now rather than waiting.”No

All three are legitimate and useful. Conflating them is not. A finding that rests on a law firm's inference is a
finding that collapses the moment someone reads the underlying memorandum.

The habit worth building this week

When commentary tells you something surprising, find the primary source that supports it before you
accept it. On Friday you will do this across three sources that disagree with one another, on a subject where the
primary sources are incomplete and moving. It is the last analytical exercise of the workshop because it is the
one that keeps everything else current after it stops being true.

**Q1.** A contractor argues that a change affecting the CMMC certification requirement means it no longer needs to implement NIST SP 800-171. Where does this reasoning fail?
  - A. It does not fail — CMMC is what makes 800-171 mandatory
    - *Feedback:* Reversed. DFARS 252.204-7012 made 800-171 contractual in 2017, seven years before the CMMC programme rule took effect. CMMC verifies an obligation that already existed.
  - B. The 800-171 obligation arises from DFARS 252.204-7012, which is a separate instrument **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* The obligation lives at link 4 of the chain; CMMC sits at links 6 to 8 as the verification layer. Changing how conformity is verified does not change the thing being verified. This is the single most useful analytical move in the field.
  - C. It fails only if the contract also contains DFARS 252.204-7021
    - *Feedback:* Backwards. The 7012 obligation is independent of 7021 — with no CMMC clause at all, 7012 still requires 800-171 implementation.
  - D. It fails because 32 CFR Part 170 cannot be amended
    - *Feedback:* Part 170 can be amended, and one day will be. The point is that a change there would not reach back and alter DFARS 252.204-7012.

**Q2.** A solicitation includes DFARS 252.204-7021 with the required CMMC status left blank, and Section L states the status corresponds to the categories of information in Section C. How is the level determined?
  - A. It cannot be determined; the offeror must ask the Contracting Officer
    - *Feedback:* Asking is always available, but here the solicitation gives you what you need. An assessor who cannot reason from the Statement of Work will be unable to do the job.
  - B. Default to Level 1 until the Government states otherwise
    - *Feedback:* Defaulting low is the most expensive error available. The information described in Section C determines the level; a blank fill-in is not a determination of Level 1.
  - C. By reading Section C to identify the information the contractor will handle, then applying 32 CFR Part 170 **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* The level follows the information. Section C describes a Technical Data Package with Distribution Statement D and export-controlled content — Controlled Technical Information, which is CUI, which is Level 2.
  - D. By the dollar value and the NAICS code
    - *Feedback:* Neither has any bearing on the CMMC level. The level is a function of the information, not of contract size or industry classification.

**Q3.** Which statement about the Statement of Work provision requiring subcontractors handling the Technical Data Package to be identified is most accurate?
  - A. It is a reporting formality with no CMMC consequence
    - *Feedback:* It is the flowdown obligation appearing in the SOW. The consequences reach every subcontractor that will touch the TDP.
  - B. It obliges the prime to flow safeguarding and certification requirements to those subcontractors **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* Flowdown is link 9 of the chain. DFARS 252.204-7012(m) requires the clause in subcontracts where performance will involve covered defense information, and the SOW states the same obligation in plain language.
  - C. It applies only to subcontractors above the simplified acquisition threshold
    - *Feedback:* There is no such threshold in the flowdown rule. The obligation follows the information, not the dollar value.

    - *Feedback:* Flowdown adds an obligation to the subcontractor; it does not discharge the prime's. Both are bound.

## Key terminology

- **Federal Contract Information** — FAR 52.204-21(a): information, not intended for public release, provided by or generated for the Government under a contract to develop or deliver a product or service. Excludes public information and simple transactional information.
- **Controlled Unclassified Information** — 32 CFR 2002.4: information the Government creates or possesses, or that an entity creates or possesses for or on behalf of the Government, that a law, regulation, or government-wide policy requires an agency to safeguard or control.
- **CUI Basic** — A category whose handling follows the uniform CUI baseline, with no additional controls set by the authorizing authority.
- **CUI Specified** — A category where the authorizing law, regulation, or policy itself sets handling requirements beyond the CUI Basic baseline. Marked with the SP- prefix, e.g. CUI//SP-CTI.
- **Designating agency** — The agency that determines information is CUI under an authority. A contractor is never one.
- **Authorized holder** — An individual, agency, organization, or group permitted to designate or handle CUI in accordance with 32 CFR Part 2002. Handling authority is not designation authority.
- **CTI** — Controlled Technical Information. Technical information with military or space application subject to controls on access, use, reproduction, modification, release, or dissemination. CUI Specified.
- **PROCURE** — General Procurement and Acquisition. Covers cost or pricing data, contract information, indirect costs, and direct labour rates. CUI Specified.
- **Medium assurance certificate** — A DoD-approved certificate required to file a cyber incident report on DIBNet. Without one, a contractor cannot report at all.
- **DIBNet** — The DoD portal through which cyber incidents are reported under DFARS 252.204-7012(c).

## 2. Who decides

This is the part practitioners get wrong most often, and the error is not subtle once you see it.

Under EO 13556 and 32 CFR Part 2002, information becomes CUI because a law, regulation, or government-wide
policy requires it to be controlled, and an authorized holder in the designating agency applies that
basis. A contractor is not a designating agency. A contractor cannot make information CUI by marking it, and cannot
make it not-CUI by declining to.

A contractor mayA contractor may not

Protect the information at the level the contract requiresDecontrol CUI — only the designating agency can

Flow the requirement down to subcontractors who will handle itRely on the absence of markings as proof that nothing is CUI

Unmarked does not mean unprotected

You will find CUI in the field carrying no marking at all — because a government office failed to mark it,
because it was extracted from a marked document, or because it was generated during performance and nobody thought
about it. The obligation attaches to the information, not to the sticker. “It was not marked” is an
explanation for how a company got into trouble. It has never been a defence.

Three details worth memorizing exactly

investigation.
90 days of media and monitoring-data preservation, running from submission of the incident report.
A medium assurance certificate is required to file the report at all. Meridian has never
obtained one, which means that on the day it needs to report it will discover this at hour 70 of a 72-hour clock.

The cloud question at Meridian, precisely

Meridian keeps CUI in Microsoft 365 GCC High and runs SBIR analysis in AWS
GovCloud. Both carry FedRAMP High authorisations — comfortably above the Moderate baseline
the clause names. So is (b)(2)(ii)(D) satisfied?

The authorisation is satisfied. The obligation may not be. Two things remain to check.

First, the clause requires the provider to comply with the cyber incident reporting, media preservation, and DoD
access requirements as well. A FedRAMP authorisation is a statement about the platform's security posture; it is not
by itself a commitment to those contractual obligations, and the assessor asks what in the agreement establishes
them.

Second — and this is where Meridian actually fails — an authorised platform that is
misconfigured is not a compliant environment. Conditional Access is only partially configured, guest access
to SharePoint is not fully restricted, and a shared service account holds Global Admin with no individual owner
assigned. The platform is doing its job. The tenant is not.

Note how this differs from the more familiar failure. A contractor keeping CUI in a commercial tenant fails at
the authorisation. Meridian passes the authorisation and fails at the configuration — which is harder to see,
easier to argue about, and far more common in companies that have spent money on the right products.

Meridian's data classification page states plainly that payroll, ESOP participant records, and commercial
supplier invoices are sensitive business data that do not meet the CUI definition. Its third-party register, three
screens away, records Whitmore & Associates — the external CPA with read-only QuickBooks access — as
handling “employee PII / CUI-Privacy,” while the same register elsewhere states the firm is
“not in SSP scope.”

Both cannot be right, and neither is authority — the Registry and the definition are. Saying what
distinguishes item 5 from item 3, since both concern employees, is the heart of the audit. The distinction is not
sensitivity. It is who the information was created for. SF-86 and DISS material is collected on the
Government's behalf under a personnel security process; a W-4 and an ESOP statement are not.

The item the company has quietly reclassified as its own

Item 4 is the interesting one. Meridian calls the SBIR research “proprietary R&D — company
confidential,” which is how a great many contractors think about work they performed. But the NAVAIR contract
identifies CUI//SP-EXPT, and the CUI definition expressly reaches information an entity creates or
possesses for or on behalf of the Government.

SBIR data rights are real and they are a different question entirely: a contractor may hold data rights in
technical data and that information may still be CUI. Rights govern what the Government may do with it. CUI status
governs how it must be protected. Conflating the two is the specific error here, and it is extremely common in
research-performing contractors.

An assessor who says “this is CUI//SP-EXPT under the contract, and your data rights position is
unaffected by that” has made the determination and defused the argument in one sentence.

Where Meridian is right

Item 2 is correct and should be marked so. Note what makes it correct: Meridian generated the FEA
reports and drawings itself, and they are still CUI, because covered defense information reaches information the
contractor develops in performance. Contractors routinely assume that only what the Government hands them counts.

## Knowledge check

**Q1.** Who may designate information as CUI?
  - A. The contractor holding it
    - *Feedback:* A contractor identifies and protects CUI. It does not designate, and it cannot decontrol. Almost every bad determination in the field is a variation on a contractor believing it holds an authority it does not hold.
  - B. Any authorized holder
    - *Feedback:* Handling authority is not designation authority. An authorized holder may possess and handle CUI in accordance with 32 CFR Part 2002 without any power to designate it.
  - C. An agency, under a law, regulation, or government-wide policy **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* Designation authority sits with the Government under EO 13556 and 32 CFR Part 2002, and it must rest on an authority that requires or permits the control.
  - D. The prime contractor, on behalf of its subcontractors
    - *Feedback:* A prime flows down obligations. It does not acquire designation authority by being higher in the supply chain.

**Q2.** Meridian classifies its SBIR final technical report as "proprietary R&D — company confidential" rather than CUI. What is the assessor's response?
  - A. Accept it — Meridian performed the research and holds data rights in it
    - *Feedback:* Conflates two separate questions. Data rights govern what the Government may do with the information; CUI status governs how it must be protected. A contractor can hold data rights in information that is also CUI.
  - B. It is CUI//SP-EXPT: the contract identifies it, and CUI reaches information created for or on behalf of the Government **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* The NAVAIR contract identifies the category, and 32 CFR 2002.4 expressly reaches information an entity creates or possesses for or on behalf of the Government. The company's data rights position is unaffected by the determination.
  - C. It is CUI only if the Government has marked the deliverable
    - *Feedback:* Marking is evidence, not the test. The obligation attaches to the information, and the contract identifies the category regardless.
  - D. It is neither — research performed at the contractor's own initiative is outside the definition
    - *Feedback:* It was not performed at the contractor's own initiative. It was performed under a $1.85M federal contract that names the CUI category.

**Q3.** Meridian keeps CUI in Microsoft 365 GCC High, which holds a FedRAMP High authorization. Its Conditional Access policies are partially configured, guest access to SharePoint is unrestricted, and a shared service account holds Global Admin. What follows?
  - A. The requirement is satisfied — FedRAMP High exceeds the Moderate baseline
    - *Feedback:* The authorization is satisfied. It is a statement about the platform, and it says nothing about how this tenant is configured.
  - B. The authorization is met, but an authorized platform that is misconfigured is not a compliant environment **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* This is the harder and more common failure. Meridian passed the procurement question and failed the implementation one — and the misconfigurations are findings against access control and configuration requirements in their own right.
  - C. GCC High is not acceptable; only a private government cloud satisfies the clause
    - *Feedback:* No such rule exists. The clause names the FedRAMP Moderate baseline or equivalent, and GCC High comfortably exceeds it.
  - D. The findings belong to Microsoft, since it operates the platform
    - *Feedback:* Tenant configuration is the customer's responsibility, not the provider's. That allocation is exactly what a Customer Responsibility Matrix exists to make explicit.

**Q4.** Within what period must a cyber incident be reported under DFARS 252.204-7012, and from when?
  - A. 24 hours from confirmation
    - *Feedback:* Neither the period nor the trigger is right.
  - B. 72 hours from discovery **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* Via DIBNet, and a DoD-approved medium assurance certificate is required to file at all.
  - C. 72 hours from completion of the investigation
    - *Feedback:* The reading contractors act on in practice, and it is wrong. Meridian acted on something close to this and then never finished the investigation either.
  - D. 30 days from discovery
    - *Feedback:* No provision of the clause supports this.

**Q5.** Meridian's payroll records, production labour hours, and commercial vendor invoices are best classified as:
  - A. CUI — Privacy
    - *Feedback:* Sensitive and regulated, but neither is the test. Ask what law requires an agency to control it. Nothing does — this material was never created for or on behalf of the Government.
  - B. FCI
    - *Feedback:* Fails the FCI definition too: it was not provided by or generated for the Government under either contract.
  - C. Neither CUI nor FCI **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* Created and held by Meridian as an employer and a business, at its own expense, for its own purposes. Expressly outside the CUI definition at 32 CFR 2002.4 and outside FCI at FAR 52.204-21(a).
  - D. CUI — PROCURE, because invoices are procurement material
    - *Feedback:* PROCURE concerns government acquisition material. Invoices from Meridian's own commercial raw-material suppliers are not that.

**Q6.** Employee clearance submissions (SF-86 data) are CUI, but ordinary payroll records are not. What distinguishes them?
  - A. Clearance data is more sensitive
    - *Feedback:* Sensitivity is not the test, and reasoning from it is how contractors end up over-marking everything.
  - B. Clearance data is collected for the Government under a personnel security process **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* The distinction is who the information was created for. SF-86 material is collected on the Government's behalf; a W-4 is not. Both concern employees; only one is inside the CUI definition.
  - C. Clearance data is classified
    - *Feedback:* CUI is unclassified by definition, and no clearance attaches to it.
  - D. Payroll is excluded because a third party processes it
    - *Feedback:* Who processes it is irrelevant to whether it is CUI. The external accountant's involvement changes the scoping analysis, not the classification.

**Q7.** Meridian classifies its contract pricing and cost data as FCI. What is the strongest assessor response?
  - A. Accept it — pricing is transactional information excluded from CUI
    - *Feedback:* The transactional exclusion in FAR 52.204-21(a) covers information necessary to process payments, not cost proposals and negotiated indirect rates.
  - B. It is arguably CUI under General Procurement and Acquisition, and at minimum is not settled **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* PROCURE covers cost or pricing data, contract information, indirect costs, and direct labour rates on its face, and it is Specified. This is the item classified too low — the direction that costs money, because the contracts folder has been handled at the FCI standard.
  - C. It is CUI under Source Selection
    - *Feedback:* Source Selection covers pre-award proposal evaluation material held by the Government. Close, and not this.
  - D. It cannot be CUI because the contractor generated it
    - *Feedback:* Contractor-generated information is squarely inside both the CUI definition and the covered defense information definition.

**Q8.** What does a banner marking of CUI//SP-CTI indicate?
  - A. The information is classified at a low level
    - *Feedback:* CUI is unclassified. Nothing about a CUI marking implies classification.
  - B. A CUI Specified category, whose authority sets handling controls beyond the CUI Basic baseline **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* The SP- prefix denotes Specified. Note that some categories are Specified under one authority and Basic under another, which is why you read the category page rather than memorizing a list.
  - C. Special permission is required to access the information
    - *Feedback:* No clearance or special permission regime attaches to CUI as such; lawful government purpose and any limited dissemination controls govern access.
  - D. The information is subject to a DoD-only control
    - *Feedback:* Specified is a government-wide construct, not a DoD one.

**Q9.** An assessor finds CUI in a contractor's SharePoint carrying no CUI markings at all. What follows?
  - A. Nothing — unmarked information is not CUI
    - *Feedback:* The obligation attaches to the information, not to the marking. This is the defence contractors reach for and it has never worked.
  - B. The contractor must mark it as CUI on its own authority
    - *Feedback:* A contractor applies required markings when it creates documents from CUI it holds. It does not designate, and marking something does not settle its status.
  - C. The information is still CUI, and the absence of marking may itself be a finding **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* Government offices fail to mark; material gets extracted from marked documents; information is generated during performance and nobody thinks about it. 252.204-7012 also reaches information "otherwise identified in the contract."
  - D. The assessor should ask the contractor to decontrol it
    - *Feedback:* Only the designating agency can decontrol CUI, and an assessor asking for anything to be changed has stopped assessing.

**Q10.** ClearPath maintains Meridian's PLM, which holds CUI, with standing remote VPN access under a master services agreement containing no security terms. Under DFARS 252.204-7012, what is missing?
  - A. Nothing — ClearPath is a vendor, not a subcontractor
    - *Feedback:* Paragraph (m) turns on whether performance will involve covered defense information, not on what the parties call the relationship.
  - B. The clause must be flowed down under paragraph (m) **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* Performance plainly involves CDI — the PLM holds it and ClearPath has standing access. The clause must be included without alteration except to identify the parties. There is also a separate maintenance requirement at MA.L2-3.7.5 for nonlocal maintenance sessions.
  - C. A FedRAMP Moderate authorization for ClearPath
    - *Feedback:* FedRAMP applies to cloud service providers. ClearPath is a non-cloud external service provider, which is a different rule with different consequences — you will separate the two on Day 3.
  - D. Nothing, provided Meridian monitors the sessions
    - *Feedback:* Monitoring would address a different requirement. It does not discharge the flowdown obligation, and in any event Meridian does not monitor them.

# Lesson 3 — Ethics, Independence, and Assessment Planning
*Day 2 &middot; Afternoon &middot; CAP Phase 1 &middot; CCP Domains 2 &amp; 5*

This is where you stop being a practitioner. An assessor who is excellent at evaluating controls and careless about independence is worse than useless &mdash; the finding is unreliable, the certificate is unsafe, and the whole verification regime is worth less for their having participated in it. Most people find this the hardest session, and not because the material is difficult.

## Learning objectives
By the end of this session you will be able to:

- **[Apply]** Apply the eight guiding principles of the CMMC Code of Professional Conduct as decision rules to concrete engagement situations, rather than recite them.
- **[Identify]** Identify conflicts of interest and impartiality threats, and distinguish those that can be mitigated from those that must be avoided.
- **[State]** State the three-year consulting prohibition accurately &mdash; what it covers, what it does not, and who it binds.
- **[Execute]** Execute the CAP preliminary proceedings and Phase 1 activities: framing, conflict management, scope validation, evidence confirmation, team composition, and the readiness determination.
- **[Produce]** Produce a defensible assessment plan with an independence declaration &mdash; including, where warranted, a determination that the engagement may not proceed.

## Key terminology

- **CoPC** — CMMC Code of Professional Conduct v2.0. Article II carries the eight guiding principles; the practices beneath each are the operative text. Article III covers conflicts of interest.
- **Preliminary proceedings** — The CAP activities that occur before Phase 1, including conflict identification and contract execution. Two of them decide whether there will be an assessment at all.
- **CAP Phase 1** — Conduct the Pre-Assessment. Asks one question: is this organization ready to be assessed? Concludes on successful upload of the Pre-Assessment Form to CMMC eMASS.
- **Three-year bar** — 32 CFR 170.8(b)(17)(ii)(G), carried into CoPC 2.2(d). Prohibits participating in the Level 2 certification process where you consulted to prepare the organization for any CMMC assessment within three years.
- **Impartiality** — The CoPC principle requiring freedom from undue commercial, personal, financial, or other pressure. The core of what makes a certificate mean anything.
- **Mitigate vs avoid** — Some conflicts can be managed by removing a person from the team. Some have no available mitigation and require the C3PAO to decline the engagement entirely.
- **Lead CCA** — The credentialed individual who validates scope, determines readiness, runs the in-brief, and owns the determination. Several CAP activities are assigned specifically to this role.
- **Pre-Assessment Form** — CAGE code, SSP title, contacts, team, dates, readiness determination. Reviewed by a C3PAO quality assurance individual and uploaded to CMMC eMASS. Not shared outside the engagement.
- **Adverse readiness determination** — A legitimate Phase 1 outcome. The Lead CCA may find the OSC not sufficiently prepared — and no one affiliated with the C3PAO may then help it get ready.
- **Customer Responsibility Matrix** — The document allocating security responsibilities between an OSC and an External Service Provider. Required where an ESP is in scope.

## 1. The shift, stated plainly

For two sessions you have been learning what the requirements are. Now your judgment about them has consequences
for another company.

That change carries obligations with nothing to do with technical skill. If you currently work as a consultant,
some of what you do routinely is prohibited to you as an assessor, and it is better to confront that now than to
discover it mid-engagement.

The distinction to carry into every remaining session

A consultant is paid to make an organization better. An assessor is paid to say accurately what is true about
it. Those are different jobs, and doing the first inside the second destroys the value of both.

## 2. The eight principles, as decision rules

The CoPC lists eight guiding principles in Article II, each with practices beneath it. The practices are the
operative text. Learn the principles as questions you ask yourself, not as a list you recite.

PrincipleThe question it asks

ProfessionalismAm I representing myself, my credentials, and my services accurately — and refraining from guarantees, low-balling, and disparagement?

ImpartialityIs there anything about my relationships, interests, or history that could compromise this determination, or reasonably appear to?

ConfidentialityIs this information leaving a boundary it was entrusted to me inside?

Information IntegrityIs what I am recording accurate, complete, and traceable to evidence?

Lawful and Ethical BehaviorDoes this comply with the law and the regulation, and not merely with the contract?

Equal OpportunityAm I applying the same standard I would apply to a larger, smaller, friendlier, or more difficult OSC?

Proper Use of CMMC MethodsAm I staying inside my role and using approved methods and materials — a CCP not encroaching on a CCA's responsibilities, for instance?

Proper Use of Technology and AIIs a tool making a determination that is mine to make — and is customer data leaving the boundary to reach that tool?

Exam tip

CoPC content is only about 5% of the CCP exam and produces a disproportionate share of the failures, precisely
because candidates assume ethics is intuition and skip the document. “Impartiality” alone is not a
citation; §2.2(g) is.

## 3. Impartiality and the three-year bar

Impartiality carries the most operative detail and ends the most careers. The CoPC is blunt about why: the
ecosystem must operate free of undue commercial, personal, financial, or other pressures, because impartiality is
the core of what makes a certificate mean anything.

State the prohibition precisely

32 CFR §170.8(b)(17)(ii)(G), carried into CoPC §2.2(d), prohibits ecosystem members from participating
in the Level 2 certification process for an assessment in which they previously served as a consultant to prepare
the organization for any type of CMMC assessment within three years.

It does coverIt does not say

Preparatory, advisory, or consulting activity of any kind, for any type of CMMC assessment
That every prior relationship with an OSC is barred for three years

Consulting for a Level 1 self-assessment two years ago, which bars you from a Level 2 certification assessment today
That the bar is the only conflict rule — most conflicts arise from other relationships entirely

The C3PAO as an organization, not only the individual assessors
That being clear of the three-year bar means you are clear to proceed

Supplying templates, workbooks, or tools that guide implementation — this is advisory activity
That you get to decide your own eligibility

The rule underneath the rule

The C3PAO owns impartiality and conflict management. The CAP says so twice, and says the
responsibility cannot be delegated to the assessment team or to the OSC. Your obligation is to disclose, in
writing, as soon as a conflict is known or reasonably should be known. The decision about whether it can be
mitigated is not yours. Assessors get into trouble far more often for deciding a conflict was fine than for having
one.

Mitigate or avoid

Some conflicts can be managed; some cannot. The CoPC's own worked examples set the pattern: a family
relationship is mitigated by removing the assessor from the team, while a C3PAO assessing an environment shaped by
its own products has no available mitigation and must decline entirely. If the conflict cannot be sufficiently
mitigated, the C3PAO does not proceed.

## 4. What happens before Phase 1

An assessment does not begin with Phase 1. The CAP places preliminary proceedings ahead of it, and two of them
decide whether there will be an assessment at all.

Preliminary activityWhat it settles

Receive the assessment requestThe OSC approaches an authorized or accredited C3PAO listed in the Marketplace. Neither the Cyber AB, the CAICO, nor DoD may recommend or introduce a C3PAO.

Confirm the entityThe precise legal entity and the CAGE code or codes. A certification attaches to an identified information system owned and operated by an identified OSC. No CAGE code, no assessment.

Frame the assessmentSchedule, size, personnel, logistics, contractual requirements, and the prospective scope, worked out with the Affirming Official or OSC point of contact.

Identify and manage conflictsThe C3PAO proposes the Lead CCA and coordinates with the OSC to establish whether conflicts exist. Documented mitigation plan required; where a conflict cannot be sufficiently mitigated, the C3PAO does not proceed.

Execute the contractual agreementA written contract and a mutual NDA. The contract may not include guarantees, promises of outcome, or any incentive or bonus contingent on a certificate being issued.

Two things students routinely get backwards

Conflicts are managed before Phase 1, not during it — and the responsibility sits with the
C3PAO, which cannot delegate it to the assessment team or to the OSC.

A contract that pays a bonus on certification, or refunds a fee if certification is not achieved, is prohibited.
If you see one in an engagement letter, that is a finding about the C3PAO, not a commercial detail.

## 5. Phase 1: Conduct the Pre-Assessment

Phase 1 asks one question: is this organization ready to be assessed? Not whether it will pass — the CAP is
explicit that the team may not speculate about or intimate a likely outcome. Only whether the evaluative work of
Phase 2 can sensibly begin.

Phase 1 activityWhat it involves

Review the SSPA cursory review for completeness, accuracy, and consistency — enough to form a reasonable expectation that the requirements have been addressed. Not an evaluation of adequacy or sufficiency; that is Phase 2.

Validate the scopeThe Lead CCA validates scope under 32 CFR §170.19(c). Disagreements about scope must be resolved before Phase 2 may begin.

Address ESPsConfirm a Customer Responsibility Matrix will be available and that ESP personnel will participate. If the ESP handles CUI, confirm the OSC can produce FedRAMP Moderate authorization, documented equivalency, or a Level 2 certificate.

Confirm evidence availabilitySatisfy yourself that enough evidence and enough people will actually be accessible to support accurate determinations.

Determine readinessThe Lead CCA makes the call and conveys which assessment methods will be used, at a depth and coverage drawn from NIST SP 800-171A and SP 800-53A.

Compose the teamThe C3PAO composes the team under 32 CFR §170.11(b)(10) and ISO/IEC 17020 personnel procedures, proposes names to the OSC, and manages conflicts before Phase 2 begins.

Complete the Pre-Assessment FormCAGE code, SSP title, contacts, team, dates, readiness determination. Not shared outside the engagement.

QA review, then uploadA C3PAO quality assurance individual reviews the form and uploads it into CMMC eMASS. Phase 1 concludes on that successful upload — not on a meeting.

And if the OSC is not ready

The Lead CCA may determine that it is not. That is a legitimate Phase 1 outcome and the paperwork is still
completed. Under no circumstances does the C3PAO, the team, or anyone affiliated with it then help the OSC get
ready — the consulting bar arriving at the moment it is hardest to honour.

## ThreadBridge — Rule on the engagement, then plan it

You are now Tidewater. The Engagement & Ethics Gate puts the packet in front of you and

## Knowledge check

**Q1.** Marcus Reyes worked at Meridian from 2019 to 2022 building the file server permission model still in use, and participated in Tidewater's gap assessment ten months ago. What is the correct disposition?

    - *Feedback:* Peer review does not cure either problem. He would still be assessing a control environment he personally built, and he still participated in the prior advisory engagement.
  - C. Avoid — remove him from the team **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* He would be assessing his own work, and he participated in an engagement that produced a Recommended Remediation Sequence. Both are impartiality failures; the second engages the three-year bar. Removal is the available mitigation for the individual, and the C3PAO decides.
  - D. No conflict — the gap assessment was non-certification work
    - *Feedback:* The bar covers preparatory or advisory activity for any type of CMMC assessment. Calling it non-certification does not remove it from scope.

**Q2.** The engagement letter provides that 30% of the fee is credited against a future assessment if no certificate issues. What is this?
  - A. A normal commercial risk-sharing term
    - *Feedback:* It is a prohibited outcome-contingent term, and reading it as ordinary commerce is exactly how it survives review.
  - B. A prohibited term — a financial interest contingent on the outcome **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* The contract may not include guarantees, promises of outcome, or any incentive or bonus contingent on a certificate being issued. If you see one in an engagement letter, that is a finding about the C3PAO.
  - C. Acceptable if disclosed to the OSC
    - *Feedback:* The OSC is party to the contract; it already knows. Disclosure does not cure a term that creates a financial stake in the determination.
  - D. Acceptable because it penalises rather than rewards certification
    - *Feedback:* It rewards certification: the firm keeps the full fee only if the certificate issues. That is a financial interest in the outcome.

**Q3.** Tidewater's prior gap assessment report included Appendix C, a "Recommended Remediation Sequence" with suggested tooling and cost estimates. What is the consequence for this engagement?
  - A. None — a gap assessment is not a CMMC assessment
    - *Feedback:* The activity, not the label, governs. Recommending a remediation sequence with tooling is advisory work to prepare the organization.
  - B. It engages the three-year bar at the organizational level **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* The bar binds the C3PAO as an organization, not only individual assessors. Tidewater consulted to prepare Meridian within three years, and Meridian built its POA&M directly from that report.
  - C. It is cured by using assessors who did not write the report
    - *Feedback:* Substituting personnel addresses the individual conflicts. It does not address a firm-level bar.
  - D. It is acceptable if Tidewater discloses it in the Pre-Assessment Form
    - *Feedback:* Disclosure is necessary and not sufficient. Where a conflict cannot be sufficiently mitigated, the C3PAO does not proceed.

**Q4.** Who decides whether an identified conflict can be sufficiently mitigated?
  - A. The individual assessor concerned
    - *Feedback:* Nobody decides their own eligibility. Assessors get into trouble far more often for deciding a conflict was fine than for having one.
  - B. The OSC, since it bears the consequence
    - *Feedback:* The OSC is coordinated with, and it does not hold the decision. Its commercial interest is precisely why it cannot.
  - C. The C3PAO **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* The C3PAO owns impartiality and conflict management, and the CAP states that the responsibility cannot be delegated to the assessment team or to the OSC.
  - D. The Cyber AB
    - *Feedback:* The Cyber AB accredits C3PAOs and would review conduct after the fact. It does not adjudicate individual engagement conflicts in advance.

**Q5.** Meridian has an SSP v2.1, ten written policies and an accurate network diagram, but no asset inventory. What does Phase 1 conclude?
  - A. Ready — the artifacts Phase 1 reviews all exist
    - *Feedback:* The checklist reading, and it misses what Phase 1 is for. An SSP that can be reviewed is necessary and not sufficient.
  - B. Not ready — scope cannot be validated under §170.19(c) without an enumeration of assets **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* The Lead CCA must validate scope, and scope disagreements must be resolved before Phase 2 may begin. Neither is possible against a list that has never been written. This is a harder call than a company with nothing, and it turns on one specific absence rather than a general impression of disorder.
  - C. Not ready — the SSP is marked "needs update"
    - *Feedback:* Closer, and it is a Phase 2 problem rather than a Phase 1 one. Phase 1's SSP review is cursory: completeness, accuracy and consistency, not currency of every control description.
  - D. Ready, provided the OSC produces an inventory during Phase 2
    - *Feedback:* Scope validation is a Phase 1 activity and disagreements must be resolved before Phase 2 begins. Deferring it inverts the process.

**Q6.** When does CAP Phase 1 conclude?
  - A. At the Phase 1 closing meeting with the OSC
    - *Feedback:* A meeting is not the trigger. This is a favourite exam distinction because it feels like it should be.
  - B. When the Lead CCA signs the readiness determination
    - *Feedback:* The determination is one activity within Phase 1, not its conclusion.
  - C. On successful upload of the quality-reviewed Pre-Assessment Form into CMMC eMASS **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* A C3PAO quality assurance individual reviews the form and uploads it. Phase 1 concludes on that upload.
  - D. When the OSC countersigns the assessment plan
    - *Feedback:* The OSC is not the gatekeeper of phase completion.

**Q7.** An assessment team member pastes SSP paragraphs and an access-control screenshot into a public AI assistant to draft finding language, edits the output, and enters it as their determination. What is the disposition?
  - A. Acceptable — the assessor reviewed and edited the output
    - *Feedback:* Editing addresses neither breach. The data has already left the boundary and the reasoning did not originate with the assessor.
  - B. A Confidentiality breach only
    - *Feedback:* Confidentiality is engaged, and so is Proper Use of Technology and AI — a tool produced reasoning that is the credentialed assessor's to produce. Information Integrity is arguably a third.
  - C. Breaches of Confidentiality and Proper Use of Technology and AI, reportable to the C3PAO **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* Engagement-confidential information describing how to attack the OSC left the boundary, and a determination was outsourced. Disclose to the C3PAO; the disposition is not the assessor's to decide.
  - D. Acceptable if the AI provider offers a no-training guarantee
    - *Feedback:* A vendor's data-handling terms do not make the disclosure authorised by the OSC, and they say nothing at all about who rendered the determination.

**Q8.** Priya Raghunathan has served two years on a professional association chapter board with Meridian's IT Manager, seeing him monthly. No financial or family relationship exists. What is the best response?
  - A. No conflict — professional association service is not a relationship of interest
    - *Feedback:* An ongoing personal relationship with a key interviewee is exactly the kind of thing that could reasonably appear to compromise impartiality.
  - B. Disclose in writing; the C3PAO determines whether it can be mitigated **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* The test is whether something could compromise the determination or reasonably appear to. Disclosure is the assessor's obligation; disposition is the C3PAO's decision. Note she also participated in the gap assessment, which is the more serious problem.
  - C. Avoid — any social relationship is disqualifying
    - *Feedback:* Overcorrecting is its own error. Not every relationship is unmitigable, and the CoPC contemplates mitigation by removal where warranted.
  - D. Mitigate by having her avoid interviewing the IT Manager
    - *Feedback:* Plausible on its face, and it addresses only the narrowest version of the problem. The decision is not hers to make in any event.

**Q9.** Thomas Blalock holds $30,000 of stock in the parent company of ClearPath, the vendor with standing remote access to Meridian's PLM. He disclosed it when he joined Tidewater. What now?
  - A. Nothing further — he already disclosed it
    - *Feedback:* A general disclosure on joining is not disclosure of a conflict in this engagement. The relationship only becomes a conflict when ClearPath appears in an assessment, and it has.
  - B. It must be disclosed for this engagement and dispositioned by the C3PAO **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* ClearPath is an ESP evaluated inside this assessment. A financial interest in its parent is a live impartiality threat, and the obligation is to disclose as soon as it is known or reasonably should be known.
  - C. No conflict — his interest is in the parent, not ClearPath
    - *Feedback:* An interest in the parent is an interest in the subsidiary's performance. Formal corporate separation does not neutralise the incentive.
  - D. He may serve provided he does not assess the ClearPath relationship
    - *Feedback:* Carving out part of the scope is not a disposition the assessor may choose, and the C3PAO decides whether any mitigation suffices.

**Q10.** In the readiness risks section of an assessment plan, which sentence is permissible?
  - A. "Meridian should implement MFA on the PLM before fieldwork begins."
    - *Feedback:* A direct instruction. Naming the fix is consulting, and it is still consulting when it appears in a risk register.
  - B. "We recommend engaging an RPO to prepare the SSP."
    - *Feedback:* Worse — it is remediation advice plus a referral, and the referral has its own CoPC exposure.
  - C. "No System Security Plan exists; Phase 1 cannot review the artifact the CAP requires." **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* States what is true and what follows procedurally. It names a risk without naming a fix. That an OSC could act on it is fine — you never told them what to do.
  - D. "The environment is close to ready; a few weeks of work should suffice."
    - *Feedback:* Two failures: it speculates about outcome, which the CAP prohibits, and it implies a remediation timeline.

# Lesson 4 — Scoping and Asset Categorization
*Day 3 &middot; Morning &middot; CCA Domain 2 (20%) &middot; CCP Scoping (15%)*

The highest-leverage decision an assessor makes. Everything downstream inherits it: the evidence you request, the interviews you schedule, the requirements you evaluate, the score you compute, and the findings you can defend. It is also where the pressure lives &mdash; scope is the one part of an assessment an organization has a direct financial interest in shrinking.

## Learning objectives
By the end of this session you will be able to:

- **[Categorize]** Categorize every asset in an environment into the five CMMC Level 2 asset categories and justify each against the defining test.
- **[Apply]** Apply the two conditional categories &mdash; Contractor Risk Managed Assets and Specialized Assets &mdash; including the conditions each depends on.
- **[State]** State the assessment boundary and document what places each asset inside or outside it.
- **[Analyse]** Analyse External Service Provider relationships under 32 CFR &sect;170.19, distinguish an ESP from a Cloud Service Provider, and say when a Customer Responsibility Matrix is required and when FedRAMP Moderate or documented equivalency is triggered.
- **[Trace]** Trace flowdown obligations to subcontractors and suppliers.

## Key terminology

- **CUI Asset** — An asset that processes, stores, or transmits CUI. Documented in the asset inventory and the SSP; assessed against all Level 2 requirements.
- **Security Protection Asset** — Provides security functions or capabilities to the assessment scope, whether or not it touches CUI. Assessed against the requirements relevant to the function it provides.
- **Contractor Risk Managed Asset** — Can, but is not intended to, process, store, or transmit CUI because of security policy, procedures, and practices in place. Conditional. SSP review primarily.
- **Specialized Asset** — Can process, store, or transmit CUI but is unable to be fully secured. Closed list: IoT, IIoT, OT, Government Furnished Equipment, Restricted Information Systems, Test Equipment. Conditional.
- **Out-of-Scope Asset** — Cannot process, store, or transmit CUI and provides no security protection for CUI Assets. The contractor must be prepared to justify the inability.
- **External Service Provider** — A non-cloud provider that processes, stores, or transmits CUI, or handles Security Protection Data. Evaluated inside the OSC's assessment; a Customer Responsibility Matrix is required.
- **Cloud Service Provider** — A provider that processes, stores, or transmits CUI via cloud services. Triggers FedRAMP Moderate authorization or documented equivalency under DFARS 252.204-7012.
- **Security Protection Data** — Data used to protect the assessment scope — configuration, log, and administrative data. A provider handling it falls inside the ESP rule even without touching CUI.
- **Assessment boundary** — The stated line around what will and will not be assessed. Named systems, with the basis for each. "The network" is not a boundary.

## 1. Where this sits in the Meridian story

Day 2 ended with a determination that Meridian was not ready and that the C3PAO could not proceed. That was
the correct outcome and it stands.

Today and tomorrow are not that assessment. They are the work that makes you capable of conducting one: scoping
an environment, planning what evidence would prove what, and rendering determinations against it. You are building
instruments and judgment on a company you now know well. Day 5 morning is where an assessment actually runs

The condition that decides most of this session

Read the Contractor Risk Managed test again: can, but is not intended to, handle CUI — because of
security policy, procedures, and practices in place. The relief depends on those existing.

Now read the Specialized Asset obligation: documented in the inventory and the SSP, shown on the network
diagram, and demonstrably managed through risk-based security policies.

Both categories are earned, not claimed. And note how little it takes to lose them. Meridian
has an SSP, ten policies and an accurate network diagram — it fails the condition on one artifact, the asset
inventory, which does not exist. One missing document closes both categories to every asset in the environment.

That is a more instructive failure than a company with nothing, because it is the one you will actually meet:
organisations that have done most of the work and cannot claim the relief because of the piece they skipped.

## 5. Justifications that work

Every categorization needs a basis that references the defining test rather than restating what the asset is.

A justification that worksA justification that does not

“Stores CUI: the /CUI contract folder resides on this volume.”“File server.”

“Provides the access control boundary for the assessment scope.”“It is a firewall, so it is a security thing.”

“Can reach the PLM through a browser on the same flat subnet; no policy prevents it.”“Production floor device, not used for CUI.”

“Cannot reach any CUI asset; on a physically separate circuit with no route to the LAN.”“Nobody uses it for CUI.”

Build the inventory before you categorize

An inventory built while categorizing becomes an argument. Assets that would be inconvenient to scope in quietly
fail to get listed, and nobody notices, because the list looks complete. List every asset first, recording what it
can reach — then categorize. It is also how you find the assets the organization forgot to mention.

## 6. Where scope errors go

A scoping error does not announce itself. It surfaces on Day 4 as a requirement nobody evaluated, on Day 5 as a
finding that cannot be supported, and in practice as a certificate that covers less than the organization believes
it does.

The asymmetry matters

Scoping an asset in that did not need to be in costs the organization time. Scoping an asset
out that should have been in produces an assessment result that is wrong. Those are not equivalent errors,
and when you are genuinely uncertain, the direction to err is not the comfortable one.

The boundary at Meridian, stated correctly

Everything at both sites is inside the boundary except Whitmore & Associates and FastenerTech. Two specific
absences put it there: no enforced segmentation, so every CORP-LAN machine reaches the CUI stores
on ENG-LAN; and no asset inventory, which closes both conditional categories to the entire
environment at once.

Note what makes this different from a company with nothing. Meridian has an SSP, ten policies, and a network
diagram. It still cannot claim the conditional treatments, because the one artifact those treatments depend on is
the one it does not have. Saying that precisely — rather than “they have no documentation” —
is the difference between a Competent and an Assessor-Ready boundary statement.

## ThreadBridge — Categorize every asset and draw the boundary

The Scope & Asset Matrix tool loads the Meridian environment asset by asset. For each
one you record what it can reach, assign a category, and write a one-line basis referencing the defining test. The
tool checks each call and tells you why — including on the five that are genuinely contested.

Then five parts, in order, because the boundary has to follow from the matrix rather than the other way round:

- The matrix — every asset, categorized, justified.

- The boundary — named systems in and out, and what places each there.

- ESP and CSP analysis — each provider classified, the resulting obligation named, and
whether a Customer Responsibility Matrix is required and exists.

- Flowdown — which suppliers must receive DFARS 252.204-7012, and on what basis.

- Open items — what you could not determine, and what evidence would settle it. There are
at least two.

Where a category is unavailable to Meridian because a condition is unmet, say so explicitly and name the
condition. And no remediation anywhere: “this asset is in scope because there is no segmentation” is
assessment; “they should segment the network” is not yours to write.

## Knowledge check

**Q1.** Meridian's CORP-LAN workstations store no CUI by design, but route freely to the ENG-LAN file server. The company has an SSP, policies and a network diagram — but no asset inventory. How are they categorized?
  - A. Contractor Risk Managed Assets — the documentation condition is substantially met
    - *Feedback:* Substantially is not the test. The treatment requires the asset to be documented in the inventory, the SSP and the network diagram. One of the three does not exist, so the category is unavailable.
  - B. Out-of-Scope Assets — the company states they hold no CUI
    - *Feedback:* Out-of-scope requires the contractor to justify the asset's inability to process, store or transmit CUI. With unsegmented routing and domain credentials, no such justification exists.
  - C. CUI Assets — the conditional category is closed by the missing inventory, so they fall back to what they can reach **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* One missing artifact closes both conditional categories to the entire environment. This is the failure you will actually meet: an organisation that has done most of the work and cannot claim the relief because of the piece it skipped.
  - D. Security Protection Assets — they sit on the corporate segment
    - *Feedback:* They provide no security function to the scope. Being on a particular subnet is not a category.

**Q2.** Which is a complete statement of the Specialized Asset list?
  - A. Any asset the contractor cannot fully secure
    - *Feedback:* That is the defining test, not the list. Both must be satisfied: the asset type must be on the list and it must be unable to be fully secured.
  - B. IoT, IIoT, Operational Technology, Government Furnished Equipment, Restricted Information Systems, Test Equipment **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* A closed list. An asset that is inconvenient to secure, old, or owned by someone else does not join it.
  - C. OT and IoT only
    - *Feedback:* Incomplete — it omits GFE, Restricted Information Systems, and Test Equipment.
  - D. Any asset located outside the primary facility
    - *Feedback:* Location has no bearing on the category.

**Q3.** Secure Shred AL collects hard-copy CUI from Meridian's Huntsville site for offsite destruction, under a standard shredding contract with no NDA. How does the regulation treat it?
  - A. Out of scope — paper is not an information system
    - *Feedback:* The most commonly missed provider in the case. CUI in hard copy is still CUI, and taking custody of it is handling it.
  - B. An ESP handling CUI, requiring a Customer Responsibility Matrix and evaluation inside the assessment **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* It takes physical custody of CUI. Contrast the Dayton arrangement, where shredding is in-house to DIN 66399 P-4 and is compliant — the same obligation, met at one site and not the other.
  - C. A Cloud Service Provider
    - *Feedback:* No cloud service is involved at any point.
  - D. Neither — destruction services are governed by MP requirements, not the ESP rule
    - *Feedback:* The media protection requirements govern how destruction must be done. They do not remove a provider that handles CUI from the ESP rule; both apply.

**Q4.** What does classifying Microsoft 365 as a CSP oblige Meridian to produce?
  - A. A Customer Responsibility Matrix
    - *Feedback:* The CRM belongs to the non-cloud ESP rule. Applying it to a CSP is the most common way the two rules get mixed up.
  - B. FedRAMP Moderate authorization or documented equivalency **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* Under DFARS 252.204-7012(b)(2)(ii)(D) — plus compliance with the reporting, preservation, and access requirements, which a commercial subscription does not impose.
  - C. A Level 2 certificate for Microsoft
    - *Feedback:* The assessment does not evaluate the cloud service itself. A Level 2 certificate is one route for an ESP handling CUI, not the CSP obligation.
  - D. Nothing — the tenant is Microsoft's responsibility
    - *Feedback:* The authorization or equivalency is evidence the OSC must produce. Responsibility for producing it does not transfer with the workload.

**Q5.** A team categorizes Meridian's backup NAS as Out-of-Scope because "it only stores backups." What is wrong?
  - A. Nothing — backup media are treated separately under MP requirements
    - *Feedback:* Media protection requirements apply to media in scope. They do not remove an asset from the scope.
  - B. Backups of CUI are CUI; the NAS stores CUI and is a CUI Asset **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* The asset organizations forget and an experienced assessor asks about first. It also has a shared local account and no offsite copy, both of which become findings once it is properly in scope.
  - C. It should be a Specialized Asset as storage infrastructure
    - *Feedback:* Storage infrastructure is not on the closed Specialized list.
  - D. It should be a Security Protection Asset because backups protect data
    - *Feedback:* Backups protect against loss; they do not provide a security function to the assessment scope in the sense the category means. It stores CUI, so it is a CUI Asset.

**Q6.** Which justification for a categorization would survive challenge?
  - A. "Production floor device, not used for CUI."
    - *Feedback:* States what the asset is for. The test asks what it can do with CUI.
  - B. "It is a firewall, so it is a security thing."
    - *Feedback:* Names the asset type and gestures at a category. It does not reference the defining test.
  - C. "Can reach the PLM through a browser on the same flat subnet; no policy prevents it." **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* References capability and the absence of the condition that would earn relief. A hostile reader wanting the boundary smaller cannot move it.
  - D. "Nobody uses it for CUI."
    - *Feedback:* Usage is not the test, and it is an assertion by the party being assessed.

**Q7.** Meridian's own inventory labels the domain controller "CUI Indirect." What should the assessor do?
  - A. Accept it — the label describes the situation accurately
    - *Feedback:* It describes it loosely, and there is no such category. Accepting invented vocabulary lets the contractor define the scope.
  - B. Recategorize it as a Security Protection Asset and note the invented category **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* It handles no CUI directly but provides authentication, group policy, and access control to the whole scope. "CUI Indirect" is not one of the five categories — a contractor inventing vocabulary, which is itself worth recording.
  - C. Recategorize it as a CUI Asset because it controls access to CUI
    - *Feedback:* Defensible only if argued from what it stores rather than from importance. The cleaner call is Security Protection Asset.
  - D. Leave it uncategorized pending the contractor's clarification
    - *Feedback:* Categorization is the assessor's determination, not something the contractor clarifies.

**Q8.** Under what circumstances does the Contractor Risk Managed Asset category become available to an OSC?
  - A. When the OSC states the asset is not intended to handle CUI
    - *Feedback:* Intention alone is not the test. The relief is earned, not claimed.
  - B. When security policy, procedures, and practices are in place that keep CUI off the asset **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* The condition is in the definition itself. Where nothing exists to show, the category is unavailable and the asset falls back to what it is capable of doing.
  - C. When the asset is on a separate VLAN
    - *Feedback:* Segmentation is strong evidence and it is not the stated condition. The definition speaks to policy, procedures, and practices.
  - D. When the asset is owned by a third party

# Lesson 5 — Evidence: Adequacy, Sufficiency, and Collection
*Day 3 &middot; Afternoon &middot; CAP Phase 2 &middot; CCP 35% / CCA 40%*

The conceptual hinge of the workshop. Everything so far has been about what applies and where. This is about proof &mdash; specifically the gap between an organization having a document and an organization doing the thing. Most people entering CMMC work close that gap without noticing. Anyone who leaves this session still equating documentation with compliance does not recover tomorrow.

## Learning objectives
By the end of this session you will be able to:

- **[Distinguish]** Distinguish adequacy from sufficiency and apply both tests to a real artifact.
- **[Decompose]** Decompose a NIST SP 800-171 requirement into its assessment objectives and identify what each objective actually demands.
- **[Select]** Select the appropriate assessment method &mdash; examine, interview, or test &mdash; and the assessment object for a given objective, and say what each method can and cannot establish.
- **[Apply]** Apply the CMMC sampling requirements for depth and coverage.
- **[Construct]** Construct an evidence request list specific enough that an organization could act on it without asking a question.
- **[Evaluate]** Evaluate an artifact and state, with reasons, whether it is adequate, sufficient, both, or neither &mdash; and what would change the verdict.

## Key terminology

- **Assessment objective** — The unit of determination. NIST SP 800-171A decomposes the 110 requirements into roughly 320 objectives. Every one must be MET or NOT APPLICABLE for the parent requirement to be MET.
- **Adequate** — The evidence exists and matches what the requirement intends. A property of the artifact, not of the organization.
- **Sufficient** — The evidence shows the control is implemented, consistently followed, and operating — across the scope, and currently.
- **Examine** — Review, inspect, study, or analyze an object. Establishes that something exists and what it says or how it is configured. Cannot establish that anyone follows it.
- **Interview** — Discuss with individuals or groups. Generates leads, not conclusions. Establishes what people understand and where practice diverges from documentation.
- **Test** — Exercise an object under specified conditions and compare actual to expected behaviour. Establishes that the control operates as intended, right now.
- **Assessment object** — What a method is applied to: specifications, mechanisms, activities, or individuals. A method is paired with an object — "we will examine" is half an answer.
- **Depth and coverage** — The CAP requires a nonstatistical sampling approach and the FOCUSED value for both depth and coverage across Level 2 requirements, increased where evidence is questionable, insufficient, or inadequate.
- **Final-form evidence** — Evidence in its completed state, as opposed to drafts or verbal assurances. What a MET determination must rest on.

## 1. Objectives, not requirements

You do not assess 110 requirements. You assess the assessment objectives those requirements decompose into
— roughly 320 across Level 2, enumerated in NIST SP 800-171A — and the arithmetic is unforgiving.

There is no partial credit

Every assessment objective under a requirement must be MET, or NOT APPLICABLE, for the parent requirement to be
MET. One objective unmet makes the whole requirement NOT MET.

This is the single most consequential mechanic in Level 2 assessment. It is why an organization that has
“mostly” implemented a control has not implemented it, and why an assessor who evaluates at the
requirement level rather than the objective level produces determinations that cannot be defended.

Take AC.L2-3.1.1 — limit system access to authorized users, processes acting on behalf of authorized
users, and devices. It looks like one idea. It is not: authorized users are identified; processes acting on
behalf of authorized users are identified; devices authorized to connect are identified; and system access is
limited to each of the three. Evidence that establishes the first three and nothing about the fourth leaves the
requirement NOT MET.

## 2. Adequacy and sufficiency

The sentence this session is built on

Adequate evidence shows the thing exists and matches what the requirement intends. Sufficient
evidence shows it is implemented, consistently followed, and operating.

A policy stating that multi-factor authentication is required is adequate. A configuration export plus a sample
of enrolled accounts is sufficient. Someone telling you it is enforced is neither.

AdequacySufficiency

The questionDoes this evidence exist, and does it match what the requirement intends?Does it show the control is implemented, consistently followed, and operating?

What to ask next“What is this a document about?”“How would I know this is still true, and true everywhere?”

The three failures you will see most

- The artifact exists but does not address the objective — a policy about passwords offered
for a requirement about session termination.

- The artifact addresses the objective but covers only part of the scope — real evidence for
one system, offered for an environment containing seven.

- The artifact is a claim rather than a record — an email saying the thing happens, a
spreadsheet built from memory, a person of unquestioned competence saying “yes, we do that.”

Exam tip

Adequacy is a property of the artifact, not of the organization. A well-drafted policy in a
non-compliant company is still adequate, and being able to say so is the discrimination that separates a Competent
assessor from one who marks everything “neither” because the company is a mess.

## 3. Methods and objects

Three methods, and each pairs with the kind of object it can actually speak to.

Depth and coverage

The CAP requires a nonstatistical sampling approach, and requires the assessment team to apply the
FOCUSED value for both depth and coverage across all Level 2 security requirements, as applicable.
The team should increase the sample when it encounters questionable, insufficient, or inadequate evidence.

Two practical consequences. Where an organization has multiple CAGE codes, all of them must be accounted for in
the sampling approach. Where it has multiple physical locations — as Meridian does — the sampling
should consider whether the locations use different physical controls and whether scans and boundaries cover all
of them.

Note the word the Assessment Guide uses: potential assessment methods and objects. The guide lists what
could establish an objective, not what must. Choosing among them is your judgment, and exercising it is
what you are being trained to do.

still find the requirement unmet will be argued out of findings in the field.

Artifact 3 — Email from the IT Manager

Two rhetorical moves are worth naming. “I have been doing this here for nine years” is an appeal to

absence of observed events is not evidence of absence when nothing is looking.

Neither — and the artifact is itself a finding. A plan of action must identify the
weakness, the corrective action, the resources required, the milestones and the completion dates. This does not

Where an artifact lives can be worse than what it says

The master copy is in a personal OneDrive on a commercial tenant, not in the authorised GCC High
environment. A document enumerating every unremediated security weakness in a defense contractor is precisely what
an adversary would target, and it is being kept outside the accredited boundary — which also contradicts the

This is the clearest example in the case of a finding that has nothing to do with the content of the document.
Ask where evidence lives, not only what it says.

Consistency check

You will need the POA&M again on Friday, when the eligibility rules in 32 CFR §170.21 turn on what a

will have a difficult Friday.

## 5. Building the request list

An evidence request list is a working instrument, not a wish list. Each line names four things.

ElementWhy it matters

The artifactSpecific enough to be retrieved. “Access control documentation” is not a request; “the current Conditional Access policy export from the Microsoft 365 tenant” is.

The methodExamine, interview, or test. This tells the organization what form the evidence has to take.

The objective it servesWhich assessment objective this is meant to establish. If you cannot name one, delete the line.

The custodianWho holds it. At Meridian this is frequently one person, which is itself a finding.

Where assessments go wrong quietly

A vague request produces a pile of documents, a week of delay, and an assessment team that starts accepting
whatever arrived because the schedule is slipping. Nobody decides to lower the standard; it lowers itself.
Specificity up front is the control.

## 6. The verdict is the least interesting part

Most assessors reach similar verdicts on these five artifacts. What separates them is the last clause —
what would change it.

An assessor who can say “this is adequate but not sufficient, and here is precisely what would make it
sufficient” has given an organization something it can act on, without ever telling it what to do. That is the
line you will walk for the rest of your career, and it is the reason every verdict in this workshop carries a
what would change it field.

The habit

Naming what evidence is missing is assessment. Telling Meridian how to produce it is not. Name the gap
precisely enough that the fix is obvious without ever being stated.

## ThreadBridge — Triage five artifacts and build the request list

The Evidence Triage tool presents the five artifacts one at a time, in full, exactly as they
were supplied. For each you name the assessment objective it is offered against, render a verdict —
adequate, sufficient, both, or neither — give the reasoning, and say what
would change the verdict. The tool then shows the model evaluation with the reasoning exposed, including the
observations that separate a Competent verdict from an Assessor-Ready one.

Part B is the request list: fifteen requirements, from AC.L2-3.1.1 through SC.L2-3.13.1, each needing one or

Every verdict must say what would change it. A verdict without that clause is incomplete, and

## Knowledge check

**Q1.** A requirement has five assessment objectives. Evidence establishes four; the fifth is not addressed by anything supplied. What is the determination?
  - A. MET, with the fifth objective noted as a weakness
    - *Feedback:* There is no MET-with-weakness determination. A noted weakness at objective level is what NOT MET means.
  - B. NOT MET **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* Every objective must be MET or NOT APPLICABLE for the parent requirement to be MET. Four out of five is NOT MET, and the finding names the objective that failed.
  - C. MET, since the preponderance of objectives is satisfied
    - *Feedback:* Preponderance has no place in this determination. Importing it from other assurance regimes is a reliable way to produce findings that collapse.
  - D. Deferred pending additional evidence
    - *Feedback:* Deferral is not a determination. You may request more evidence during the assessment; at determination time the answer is MET, NOT MET, or NOT APPLICABLE.

**Q2.** Meridian supplies a current Conditional Access export showing MFA enforced for all 163 users on all cloud apps, with one shared Global Admin service account excluded. The SSL VPN has no MFA. How do you evaluate the artifact against IA.L2-3.5.3?

  - B. Neither — the requirement is NOT MET
    - *Feedback:* Rejecting good evidence because the requirement fails is the mirror-image error. Adequacy is a property of the artifact.
  - C. Adequate, and sufficient only for the Microsoft 365 boundary; the requirement is NOT MET **(correct)**

  - D. Adequate and sufficient, with the other systems handled as a separate finding
    - *Feedback:* The other systems are not a separate requirement. They are within the scope of this one, and the determination has to reflect that.

**Q3.** Which best describes what an interview can establish on its own?
  - A. That a control is implemented, if the interviewee is credible and knowledgeable
    - *Feedback:* Competence and honesty are not evidence. The assessments that go wrong go wrong with likeable people.
  - B. Nothing on its own — it generates leads to be corroborated **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* An interview establishes what people understand and where practice diverges from documentation. It is a lead, not a conclusion.
  - C. That a policy exists
    - *Feedback:* Existence of a document is established by examining it, not by being told about it.

    - *Feedback:* Nothing establishes past operation from a conversation. Even a test only establishes current operation.

  - B. Adequate but not sufficient — it needs supporting review records

  - B. Adequate but not sufficient **(correct)**

  - C. Sufficient but not adequate

  - D. Adequate and sufficient, with a separate finding for non-compliance
    - *Feedback:* Non-compliance is not a separate finding. It is what makes the evidence insufficient.

  - A. The severity of the associated finding
    - *Feedback:* Severity attaches to findings, and it follows from the requirement and the exposure, not from the evidence evaluation.
  - B. A recommendation for how the OSC could produce better evidence
    - *Feedback:* That is remediation advice. Naming what is missing is assessment; telling the OSC how to produce it is not.
  - C. What would change the verdict **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* An assessor who can say precisely what would make an artifact sufficient has given the organization something it can act on without ever telling it what to do. A verdict without that clause is incomplete.
  - D. The name of the assessor who evaluated it
    - *Feedback:* Useful for records, and not the substantive element the evaluation turns on.

# Lesson 6 — Assessing the Fourteen Domains
*Day 4 &middot; Morning &middot; CAP Phase 2 &middot; CCP 35% / CCA 40%*

Everything you have built &mdash; the boundary, the evidence discipline, the objective-level thinking &mdash; gets pointed at fourteen families of requirements and turned into determinations. Every small contractor fails in roughly the same places. Knowing the pattern tells you where to look first. It does not tell you what you will find.

## Learning objectives
By the end of this session you will be able to:

- **[Assess]** Assess requirements across the fourteen NIST SP 800-171 Rev 2 families using the CMMC Assessment Guide for Level 2.
- **[Render]** Render and justify MET, NOT MET, and NOT APPLICABLE determinations at the assessment-objective level.
- **[Recognize]** Recognize the characteristic failure pattern of each domain in a small-contractor environment.
- **[Defend]** Defend a determination against challenge, arguing from the objectives rather than from experience or seniority.
- **[Detect]** Detect where a gap has been mapped to the wrong requirement, and argue the correct mapping.

## Key terminology

- **MET** — All applicable assessment objectives are satisfied by final-form evidence. An enduring exception with documented mitigation, or a temporary deficiency covered by an operational plan of action, can still be MET.
- **NOT MET** — One or more objectives is not satisfied. The finding must name which objective and what is missing.
- **NOT APPLICABLE** — The requirement does not apply to the assessment scope. Treated as MET for scoring purposes, which is exactly why it attracts pressure.
- **Enduring exception** — A special circumstance where remediation is not feasible — documented in the SSP with a mitigation. Not the same as a deficiency.
- **Operational plan of action** — An OSC-managed plan covering a temporary deficiency, documented in the SSP. Distinct from the POA&M used for conditional status.
- **AC / AU / CM / IA** — Access Control, Audit and Accountability, Configuration Management, Identification and Authentication — four of the fourteen families, and where the heaviest point values cluster.

## 5. N/A discipline

NOT APPLICABLE scores as MET. That makes it the determination an OSC has the strongest incentive to invite, and
the one you should be slowest to grant.

Meridian's own SPRS submission claimed three:

- 3.13.3 — “Not applicable, single site network.” The company has two sites,
in different states, on one flat LAN. The premise is false on the company's own facts.

- 3.2.2 — “Not applicable, training is delivered by a third party.” Who delivers
training has no bearing on whether the requirement applies. Outsourcing execution does not outsource the
obligation.

- 3.10.3 — “Not applicable, no visitors access the server room.” An assertion by
the party being assessed, contradicted by a warehouse with no visitor log where delivery drivers come and go.

Recognize when N/A is being invited

None of these three is a considered determination. Each is a reason not to do work, written by someone who
needed a number. An assessor who accepts them has allowed the OSC to score itself.

## 6. Defending a determination

Challenges are arbitrated against the Assessment Guide text, not by assertion and not by seniority. If a
determination cannot be defended from the objectives, it changes.

This cuts both ways, and the second direction is the one people find harder. A team that never concedes anything
has stopped assessing and started defending. Distinguish new information, which should change your
call, from pressure, which should not.

Lead with the call you are least sure of

The instinct is to present your best work. That wastes the exercise: nobody learns anything from a determination
everyone agrees with. A categorization or determination that cannot survive a peer challenge will not survive an
organization that stands to save money by defeating it.

# Lesson 7 — Scoring, SPRS, and the Incident Clock
*Day 4 &middot; Afternoon &middot; DoD Assessment Methodology &middot; DFARS 252.204-7012*

Determinations become a number. The number is posted to SPRS, read by a contracting officer, and affirmed under signature by a named official. An arithmetic slip here is not a rounding error &mdash; it is a false statement in a federal system. Then a device goes missing, and you decide whether a 72-hour clock has started.

## Learning objectives
By the end of this session you will be able to:

- **[Calculate]** Calculate a NIST SP 800-171 DoD Assessment score using the 5, 3, and 1 point weighting, from a maximum of 110 to a floor of &minus;203, including the two partial-credit exceptions.
- **[Distinguish]** Distinguish the DoD Assessment score from the CMMC certification determination, and say what each is for.
- **[Explain]** Explain what a posted SPRS score commits a company to, and who personally carries that commitment.
- **[Rule]** Rule on whether an incident triggers the DFARS 252.204-7012 72-hour obligation, state when the clock starts, and defend the decision against the strongest counter-argument.
- **[Identify]** Identify what a contractor must hold in order to file a report at all.

## Key terminology

- **DoD Assessment Methodology** — The instrument that produces the SPRS score. Start at 110 and deduct 5, 3, or 1 per unimplemented requirement. A strategic assessment instrument — not the CMMC certification assessment.
- **Basic / Medium / High assessment** — The three DoD Assessment types under DFARS 252.204-7020. Basic is a contractor self-assessment; Medium and High are government-conducted, at increasing rigour.
- **The floor** — −203. What you get by implementing nothing: the weighted deductions total far more than 110. Negative scores are not computational errors and contractors do post them.
- **Partial credit** — Exists for exactly two requirements — 3.5.3 (multifactor authentication) and 3.13.11 (FIPS-validated encryption). Everywhere else the deduction is all or nothing.
- **Cyber incident** — Actions that result in an actual or potentially adverse effect on an information system or the information residing in it. The threshold is potentially adverse.
- **Rapid report** — The 72-hour report to DoD via DIBNet required by DFARS 252.204-7012(c)(1), running from discovery.
- **Assessment of record** — The current DoD Assessment posted in SPRS, which DFARS 252.204-7019 and -7020 make a condition of award.

## 1. Two different things that both look like scoring

Keep these separate from the first minute

A CMMC assessment determination is binary at the objective level. Every objective is MET or NOT
APPLICABLE, or the requirement is NOT MET. There is no partial credit.

The DoD Assessment Methodology score is a weighted 110-point scale with deductions of 5, 3, or 1
— and two requirements where partial credit does exist.

These are different instruments serving different purposes, and candidates conflate them every year.

The methodology exists because DoD needed a way to compare the security posture of thousands of contractors at
acquisition time, quickly, from self-reported data. It is a strategic risk instrument. A CMMC certification
assessment is a conformity determination about one organization at one moment. A company can hold a respectable
SPRS score and fail a certification assessment, and the reverse is arithmetically possible too.

## 2. The weighting, and why it is what it is

Start at 110 and subtract. The weighting reflects what an unimplemented requirement would let an attacker do
— not how hard the requirement is to satisfy.

ValueWhat it coversExamples

5 pointsRequirements whose absence renders other controls ineffective or permits significant exploitation or exfiltrationLimiting system access to authorized users; establishing baseline configurations; incident-handling capability; controlling removable media; vulnerability scanning

3 pointsRequirements with a specific and confined security effectLimiting access to CUI on system media; encrypting CUI on mobile devices; least privilege; tracing actions to individual users

1 pointThe remaining derived requirements, with limited or indirect effectPreventing reuse of identifiers for a defined period; developing and updating the System Security Plan

The two partial-credit exceptions — and only these two

RequirementDeduct 3Deduct 5

3.5.3 — Multifactor authenticationMFA implemented for remote and privileged users, but not for the general userMFA not implemented for any users

3.13.11 — FIPS-validated encryptionEncryption is employed but is not FIPS validatedEncryption is not employed

Every other requirement is all or nothing. The temptation to award partial credit elsewhere, because an
organization has “mostly” done something, is the most common scoring error there is.

The floor

The maximum is 110. The minimum is −203, which is what you get by implementing nothing:
the weighted deductions total far more than 110. A negative score is not a computational error, and contractors do
post them.

## 3. The 3.5.3 trap, worked

Meridian's gap G-01 reads: MFA on Microsoft 365 only; PLM, file server, domain controller RDP, VPN,
QuickBooks, and the backup NAS all single-factor. What is the deduction?

A substantial minority answer 3, reasoning that MFA is partly implemented. Read the exception
precisely: the 3-point treatment requires MFA for remote and privileged users. Meridian has
neither — the SSL VPN is remote access with no MFA, and domain controller RDP is privileged access with no
MFA. Microsoft 365 alone earns nothing under the exception.

Deduct 5.

Exam tip

The exception is a conjunction, not a spectrum. “Some MFA exists” is not the test. Ask specifically:
is MFA in place for remote users? Is it in place for privileged users? Both yes and the general user missing is a
3. Either one missing is a 5.

## 6. The incident clock: INC-003

Wednesday, 09:40

Trevor Nakamura, reconciling VPN accounts against the HR separation list in preparation for the assessment, finds
that one of the six stale accounts — jhollis, belonging to a design engineer who left in November
— shows successful VPN authentications on four dates over the past six weeks, the most recent
eleven days ago. Each session lasted between twenty and ninety minutes.

Known: the account was never disabled. It held membership in the engineering security group,
granting read and write access to the CUI tree on SYS-003 and to the Arena PLM. The VPN has no MFA. The password was
last set fourteen months ago.

Not known: who authenticated. Whether anything was accessed, opened or copied. Whether the
sessions came from the former employee, from someone using credentials he left behind, or from an unrelated party.
The FortiGate retains authentication events; SYS-003 and SYS-004 retain 30 days and are not reviewed, so file-level
records for three of the four sessions have already rolled off.

Nakamura’s position: “It is almost certainly Jared. He was finishing a handover and
he is a good guy. Nothing has been taken — we would see it.”

The determination

QuestionAnswer

Does it trigger?Yes. A cyber incident includes actions
resulting in an actual or potentially adverse effect on an information system or the information residing in
it. Unauthorised authentication to a network holding CUI, by an account that should not exist, with write access to
the CUI store, is not merely potential — it is actual unauthorised access. The open question is only what was
done with it.

Which paragraph252.204-7012(c)(1) for the rapid report; the definition of cyber
incident at (a) supplies the threshold. Media preservation under (e) attaches once reported — urgently here,
because logs are actively rolling off.

When the clock starts72 hours from discovery — Wednesday 09:40, when
Nakamura identified the authentications. Not from the first session six weeks ago, and not from the separation date.
Document the gap: a contracting officer will ask how an account authenticated four times unnoticed, and the answer is
evidence about the monitoring capability rather than about the clock.

Who decidesNot Nakamura. There is an IRP naming an Incident Commander, and it is
three years stale and lists a departed employee — so in practice the CISO and the CEO, who is also the
Authorizing Official, must be involved. That the decision has nowhere to go is itself a finding under
IR.L2-3.6.1.

What preservation now requiresImages of affected systems and relevant monitoring
data for at least 90 days from the report. With 30-day retention and no review, the preservation obligation
is already partly impossible to satisfy for the earlier sessions. Preserving what remains is urgent, and it
is the first thing an assessor would ask whether anyone did.

The counter-argument, and the answer

Nakamura’s position deserves to be taken seriously before it is dismantled: it is almost certainly the
former employee finishing a handover; there is no evidence of exfiltration; and reporting an incident involving a
trusted ex-colleague feels disproportionate.

The threshold is “potentially adverse,” not “malicious,” and reporting is not an
accusation. An authorised handover would have been performed on an authorised account. And “almost certainly
Jared” is an inference about identity the environment cannot support: with no MFA, the authentication proves
possession of a credential and nothing whatever about who held it.

His second claim — “nothing has been taken, we would see it” — is an appeal to the absence
of evidence. Ask whether Meridian has the capability to have observed what he says has not happened. It does not: no
file-access review, no SIEM, no correlation, and 30-day retention that has already consumed most of the record.

Could this organization know what it claims to know? That is the question you will ask for the rest of
your career.

    - *Feedback:* Failing to submit a score is a contractual violation, not a scored one. Students who deduct for it have not understood what the instrument measures.
  - D. It cannot be scored; the assessment is invalid without a prior score
    - *Feedback:* A missing prior score does not invalidate an assessment.

**Q4.** A contractor implements nothing at all. What score does the DoD Assessment Methodology produce?
  - A. 0
    - *Feedback:* A natural guess, and the scale does not floor at zero.
  - B. −110
    - *Feedback:* That would be the arithmetic if every requirement were worth 1 point. They are not.
  - C. −203 **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* The weighted deductions total far more than 110. A negative score is not a computational error, and contractors do post them.
  - D. The score is undefined below zero
    - *Feedback:* It is well defined, and −203 is the stated floor.

**Q5.** A stale VPN account belonging to a separated employee is found to have authenticated four times over six weeks; the most recent session was eleven days ago and the discovery is made Wednesday at 09:40. When does the 72-hour clock start?
  - A. At the first session six weeks ago
    - *Feedback:* The clause runs from discovery, not from occurrence. The six-week gap belongs in the timeline as evidence about monitoring, and it does not move the clock.
  - B. Wednesday at 09:40, on discovery **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* 72 hours from discovery. Document the six-week gap: a contracting officer will ask how an account authenticated four times unnoticed, and the answer speaks to the monitoring capability rather than to the deadline.
  - C. When forensic analysis confirms whether CUI was accessed
    - *Feedback:* Confirmation is not the trigger. This is the reading contractors act on, and it is wrong — here it is also impossible, since most of the file-access record has already rolled off.
  - D. It does not start — the sessions were probably the former employee
    - *Feedback:* The threshold is a potentially adverse effect. And with no MFA, the authentication proves possession of a credential and nothing about who held it.

**Q6.** Nakamura argues no report is needed because "nothing has been taken — we would see it." What is the assessor's strongest response?
  - A. He may be right, so the decision should be deferred pending investigation
    - *Feedback:* Deferring past 72 hours is itself the violation, and the clock does not pause for an investigation.
  - B. The organization has no capability to have observed what he says has not happened **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* No PLM session logging, no log review, no defined retention. His position rests on an inference from absence of evidence, and absence of evidence proves nothing when nothing is looking.
  - C. His seniority does not make him a designated decision-maker
    - *Feedback:* True, and it is a separate point. The substantive answer is that his claim is unsupportable on the organization's own capability.
  - D. Reporting is mandatory for all lost devices regardless of circumstances
    - *Feedback:* Overstated. The test is whether the incident meets the clause threshold, and here it does — but not because every lost device does.

**Q7.** What must Meridian hold in order to file a cyber incident report at all?
  - A. A DIBNet account and a CAGE code
    - *Feedback:* Necessary and not sufficient. One further item is required and Meridian has never obtained it.
  - B. A DoD-approved medium assurance certificate **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* Without it the report cannot be filed. Meridian has never obtained one, which means it will discover this at hour 70 of a 72-hour clock.
  - C. A completed forensic report
    - *Feedback:* The rapid report precedes forensics. Waiting for analysis is how the clock is missed.
  - D. Written authorisation from the Contracting Officer
    - *Feedback:* No such authorisation is contemplated; the obligation runs to the contractor.

**Q8.** Which best distinguishes the DoD Assessment score from a CMMC certification determination?
  - A. They are the same instrument, expressed differently
    - *Feedback:* Conflating them is the error this session exists to prevent.
  - B. The score is a weighted strategic risk instrument; the certification determination is a binary conformity determination at the objective level **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* Different purposes, different mechanics, different consequences. A company can hold a respectable score and still fail certification — and two of the requirements that block certification are worth one point each.
  - C. The score applies to Level 1 and the determination to Level 2
    - *Feedback:* The DoD Assessment Methodology concerns the 110 NIST requirements, which is Level 2 territory.
  - D. The score is produced by the C3PAO and the determination by DoD
    - *Feedback:* Reversed and wrong in both directions. The score is typically a contractor self-assessment; the certification determination is the C3PAO's.

**Q9.** A student computes Meridian's score as 59 and stops. What is missing?
  - A. Nothing — 59 is the correct figure from the gap list
    - *Feedback:* It is the correct arithmetic and an incomplete answer. A bare number is what gets posted to SPRS in the real world.
  - B. A statement that 59 is an optimistic ceiling, since eight seeded findings are not an assessment of 110 requirements **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* A real assessment examines all 110 and would find more NOT MET — media disposal, Dayton physical access, and the uncontrolled AWS upload path are three the list never reaches. Also worth stating: the score is not the only gate, because a POA&M-ineligible requirement is already failing.
  - C. Conversion of the score to a percentage
    - *Feedback:* 59 ÷ 110 = 0.536 matters for the POA&M threshold, and it is arithmetic rather than the missing insight.
  - D. A comparison to the industry average
    - *Feedback:* No such benchmark governs anything in the methodology.

**Q10.** Marcus Ellison affirmed a score of 88 because "Dennis and the consultant said that was the number." What is the assessor's finding?
  - A. No finding — the Affirming Official may rely on technical staff
    - *Feedback:* Reliance is not the standard. The affirmation is a present-tense attestation of continued compliance by someone with authority to bind the organization.
  - B. The affirmation is a present-tense attestation she did not verify, and the exposure attaches to her personally **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* An affirmation that is not true is a false statement in a federal system with False Claims Act consequences for the signer. Nobody behaved dishonestly, the number is still wrong, and she is still the person who attested to it.
  - C. The consultant is liable for the incorrect figure
    - *Feedback:* A consultant cannot bind the OSC and does not carry the affirmation exposure.
  - D. The finding belongs against the IT Manager for supplying the number
    - *Feedback:* Supplying a wrong number is a contributing failure. The attestation, and its exposure, sit with the signer.

# Lesson 8 — Conducting the Assessment and Reporting Findings
*Day 5 &middot; Morning &middot; CAP Phases 2&ndash;3 &middot; The mock assessment*

The summative session. No lecture. Eight months have passed inside the case: the last C3PAO declined the engagement and &mdash; correctly &mdash; gave Meridian no help getting ready. The company hired a consultant, worked its plan, spent about $210,000, and engaged a different firm. You are that firm. Some of what you are about to read is real. Some of it is a document produced to close a line on a plan.

## Learning objectives
By the end of this session you will be able to:

- **[Execute]** Execute a Level 2 assessment against a supplied evidence package under time constraint.
- **[Run]** Run the in-brief, daily checkpoint, and out-brief structure that CAP Phases 2 and 3 require.
- **[Conduct]** Conduct interviews that elicit evidence rather than confirmation.
- **[Write]** Write an evidence request that returns what you actually need.
- **[Render]** Render final determinations and identify the findings that follow from them.
- **[Produce]** Produce a findings report that is defensible against the Assessment Guide when challenged.

## Key terminology

- **In-brief** — The CAP-prescribed opening of Phase 2. The Lead CCA must cover a defined minimum, and minutes or a detailed summary must be kept.
- **Daily checkpoint** — Required at the end of each assessment day: progress, challenges, and what is needed next. Not a negotiation and not a preview of the result.
- **Out-brief** — Delivery of assessment results. Results must be compiled and quality-reviewed beforehand by a CCA not on the team who has had no contact with it during the assessment.
- **Re-evaluation window** — Ten business days, under 32 CFR §170.17, in which limited deficiencies may be re-evaluated.
- **Finding** — What you write about a NOT MET: which objective failed, what evidence you examined, what was missing, and a severity.
- **Not provided** — One of the three outcomes for every requested item — alongside received-and-responsive and received-and-not-responsive. The third category is a finding in its own right.

## 1. Two rules for the session

Non-negotiable

You may not tell Meridian how to fix anything. Not in the checkpoint, not in an interview,
not in the out-brief, and not when the chief executive asks you directly and reasonably. She will.

You may not speculate about the outcome. Not “this is looking good,” not “you
are close.” The CAP prohibits it in Phase 1 and the reasoning holds throughout: an assessment result is a
determination, not a running commentary.

## 2. Running the in-brief

The CAP sets out what the Lead CCA must address at minimum. Your in-brief is assessed on whether you covered it.

- Introduce the assessment team and invite the OSC to introduce its people.

- Confirm the CMMC assessment scope.

- Explain the Level 2 assessment procedures under 32 CFR §170.17(c).

- Review the assessment schedule.

- Reconfirm the absence of, or disclose, any organizational or individual conflicts of interest.

- Inform the OSC of its right to appeal and describe the C3PAO's appeals process.

- Invite questions.

Minutes or a detailed summary must be kept. Nominate someone to take them before you start.

The checkpoint

A daily checkpoint with the OSC point of contact is required at the end of each assessment day: progress,
challenges, and what you need next. It is not a negotiation and it is not a preview of the result.

The OSC will push back, and pushing back is legitimate — an organization that accepts every preliminary
finding without question is not paying attention. Your job is to distinguish new information, which
should change your call, from pressure, which should not.

## 5. The overnight evidence request

You have one written request and limited time. Everything you fail to ask for, you will not have.

- Name artifacts, not topics. Day 3 applies here exactly.

- Say which objective each item serves — it forces you to check that you need it.

- Name the custodian. At Meridian this is often the same person, which is itself worth noting.

- Ask for what would disconfirm the OSC's position, not only what would confirm it.

Read what comes back, not what you hoped would come back

Some of the supplemental material will be responsive. Some will not be. Some of what you asked for will not
exist. For every item you requested, record one of three outcomes: received and responsive,
received and not responsive, or not provided.

A partly responsive answer is the normal case in a real assessment, and it is where assessments quietly fail. A
document arrives, it is roughly on the subject, the schedule is tight, and it gets accepted. The third category is
a finding in its own right.

## 6. Determinations, findings, and the out-brief

A determination is MET, NOT MET, or NOT APPLICABLE at the objective level. A
finding is what you write about a NOT MET: which objective failed, what evidence you examined, and
what was missing. Severity follows from the requirement and the exposure, not from how annoyed you are about it.

Rule on both incidents in Meridian's history — the October 2023 spearphishing that CrowdStrike blocked,
and the February 2024 lost unencrypted USB drive — and on anything the interviews surface. One of the two
triggers the DFARS 252.204-7012 obligation and one is genuinely arguable. If you rule the same way on both without
distinguishing them, read them again. Note also that the company filed nothing on either, and that the
non-reporting decisions were made informally with no documented analysis against the clause.

Quality assurance before the out-brief

The CAP requires assessment results to be compiled and quality-reviewed before the out-brief, and requires the
quality reviewer to be a CCA who is not on the assessment team and who has had no contact
with it during the assessment.

Deliver the results. Do not deliver advice. Expect at least one challenge, and be ready to concede — a team
that never concedes anything has stopped assessing and started defending.

What the report is really for

Whether a stranger could act on it. A contracting officer, an appeals panel, or the next assessment team should
be able to read it and know exactly what you looked at, what you concluded, and why — without you in the room
to explain.

## ThreadBridge — Run the assessment, write the findings

The case workbench holds the full evidence package, the interview personas, the incident records, and the
findings workspace. Work the two-day sequence in one session:

- In-brief — cover the CAP minimum. Nominate a minute-taker first.

- Evidence review — nine documents, logged as you go. Do not leave determinations to memory.

- Checkpoint — brief preliminary findings. Expect pushback on at least one call.

- Evidence request — one page, artifacts not topics, with the objective and custodian named.

- Interviews — choose who, and be able to say why. Open questions only.

- Determinations and incident rulings — objective level, evidence cited.

- Out-brief — results, not advice. Concede where the evidence moves you.

Then write the Assessment Findings Report: scope statement, methodology including sampling
values, determinations with evidence cited, findings with severity, incident analysis with clause reasoning, and a
preliminary score with the outcome you would recommend.

Every finding traceable to evidence you actually examined. If you did not see it, you cannot cite
it. Where evidence was requested and not provided, say so — absence of evidence is a result and belongs in the
report. And no remediation advice anywhere, including in the recommendation section.

## Knowledge check

**Q1.** A POA&M item is marked "Complete" with no closure evidence attached, while the MFA Status Report shows the underlying gap still open. What is the determination on the affected requirement?
  - A. MET — the POA&M records the item as closed
    - *Feedback:* A POA&M is the organization's record of its own intentions. Marking an item complete is a claim, not evidence.
  - B. NOT MET, and the POA&M discrepancy is itself worth recording **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* Three documents disagree about one fact: the SSP claims implementation, the POA&M claims closure, and the status report shows the gap open. The status report is the artifact with the most direct evidentiary value, and the inconsistency belongs in the findings.
  - C. Deferred pending confirmation from the OSC
    - *Feedback:* There is no deferred determination, and the OSC's confirmation would be another assertion.
  - D. MET with a documented deficiency
    - *Feedback:* That treatment requires a temporary deficiency covered by an operational plan of action documented in the SSP. An unevidenced "Complete" is not that.

**Q2.** Who may perform the quality assurance review of assessment results before the out-brief?
  - A. The Lead CCA who ran the assessment
    - *Feedback:* The reviewer must be independent of the assessment team, which the Lead CCA plainly is not.
  - B. Any CCP employed by the C3PAO
    - *Feedback:* The role requires a CCA, not a CCP.
  - C. A CCA not on the assessment team who has had no contact with it during the assessment **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* Both conditions matter. Independence of composition and independence of contact are separate requirements, and the second is the one people forget.
  - D. The OSC's Affirming Official
    - *Feedback:* The OSC does not quality-review the assessment of itself.

**Q3.** Meridian's Incident Response Plan names three response team members, two of whom left the company more than a year ago, and records no exercise. Against IR.L2-3.6.1, what follows?
  - A. MET — an operational incident-handling capability is documented
    - *Feedback:* Documented is not the test. The requirement is a capability, and a plan naming people who do not work there does not establish one.
  - B. NOT MET — the plan does not evidence an operational capability **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* A plan that has never been exercised and assigns roles to departed staff is adequate as a document and insufficient as evidence of capability. The contradiction between the plan and the personnel records is the assessable content.
  - C. NOT APPLICABLE — the company has had no reportable incidents
    - *Feedback:* It has had two, and in any event the requirement does not turn on whether incidents have occurred.
  - D. MET, with the staffing gap recorded as an observation
    - *Feedback:* An observation does not repair a NOT MET, and the failure here is the absence of a capability rather than a staffing detail.

**Q4.** The ClearPath addendum incorporates DFARS 252.204-7012 in full, but no Customer Responsibility Matrix accompanies it and remote access remains via a shared VPN account with no logging. What is the assessor's analysis?
  - A. Flowdown is complete; the access arrangement is a separate finding

  - B. Flowdown is satisfied, but the ESP treatment is incomplete without a CRM, and shared unlogged access implicates MA.L2-3.7.5 **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* Three threads: the clause is flowed down; the ESP documentation is incomplete; and nonlocal maintenance without MFA, session control, or records is a distinct requirement failure. Mehta believes signing the addendum completed the obligation, and that belief is itself worth recording.
  - C. The addendum is void because it postdates the CUI handling
    - *Feedback:* Nothing makes it void. It is a late but effective flowdown.
  - D. No finding — ClearPath is out of scope as an external party
    - *Feedback:* An ESP handling CUI is evaluated inside the OSC's assessment. It is squarely in scope.

**Q5.** For an item you requested and received something roughly on the subject but not addressing the objective, what should the evidence log record?
  - A. Received
    - *Feedback:* Insufficiently precise, and it is how partly responsive material quietly becomes accepted material.
  - B. Received and not responsive **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* One of the three outcomes, alongside received-and-responsive and not-provided. This is where assessments quietly fail: a document arrives, it is roughly on the subject, the schedule is tight, and the standard lowers itself.
  - C. Not provided
    - *Feedback:* Something was provided, and recording otherwise misstates what happened.
  - D. Pending
    - *Feedback:* Pending is a status during the assessment, not an outcome in the log.

**Q6.** Marcus Ellison asks the team: "Just tell me what you need to see and we will go and do it." What is the correct response?
  - A. Explain what evidence would satisfy the outstanding objectives — it is a request for clarity, not advice
    - *Feedback:* It is the most tempting wrong answer in the exercise, because it sounds like process rather than remediation. Telling the OSC what to produce is directing its remediation.
  - B. Decline, without lecturing, and continue the assessment **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* A team that answers has failed regardless of how good its determinations are. Declining gracefully — no speech about independence, no implied criticism — is the skill being assessed.
  - C. Decline and explain at length why the CoPC prohibits it
    - *Feedback:* The declining is right and the lecture is not. She is being reasonable, and a sermon damages the engagement without improving the determination.
  - D. Refer her to the C3PAO's project manager
    - *Feedback:* Passing the request along does not answer it and implies someone at the firm might say yes.

**Q7.** Which interview question is most likely to produce evidence rather than confirmation?
  - A. "You review the audit logs monthly, correct?"
    - *Feedback:* A yes-or-no invitation to agree. He will say yes, it will be true as he understands it, and you will have learned nothing.
  - B. "Walk me through the last review you did. What did you open?" **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* Open, specific, and anchored to an actual event. This is the question that reveals the "monthly review" is five or ten minutes on a firewall dashboard with the Windows event logs never opened.
  - C. "Are you satisfied that logging is adequate?"
    - *Feedback:* Invites an opinion about adequacy, which is the assessor's determination to make, not the interviewee's.
  - D. "Do you have a log retention policy?"
    - *Feedback:* Answerable yes or no, and it tells you nothing about what is retained or for how long.

**Q8.** Why is interviewing Diana Kowalczyk at the Huntsville warehouse often the better assessment decision?
  - A. He is the most senior person available at the second site
    - *Feedback:* Seniority is not the criterion, and he is a warehouse supervisor.
  - B. He can tell you things no document records and nobody else thinks matter **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* An unchanged door code given verbally to seasonal staff, no visitor log, shared warehouse logins, an unaccounted-for spare laptop in a drawer. Almost no team chooses him, which is exactly why the material is still there.
  - C. He is the custodian of the Huntsville asset inventory
    - *Feedback:* There is no Huntsville asset inventory, which is part of the problem.
  - D. His account will corroborate the SSP boundary statement
    - *Feedback:* It will contradict it, which is the point — but corroboration is not why you would choose him.

**Q9.** A team's findings report cites an artifact the team requested but never received. What is wrong?
  - A. Nothing, provided the OSC confirmed the artifact exists
    - *Feedback:* Confirmation that something exists is not examination of it.
  - B. Every finding must be traceable to evidence actually examined; not-provided is recorded as a result, not cited as evidence **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* If you did not see it, you cannot cite it. Absence of evidence is a legitimate result and belongs in the report as such.
  - C. The artifact should be cited with a note that it was unavailable
    - *Feedback:* Citing an unexamined artifact, however annotated, misrepresents the basis of the determination.
  - D. The finding should be withdrawn
    - *Feedback:* The finding may be perfectly sound on other evidence. What must change is the citation.

**Q10.** Which section of a CAP Phase 3 findings report is most often written badly, and what does good look like?
  - A. The scope statement — it should list every asset individually
    - *Feedback:* Exhaustive asset listing belongs in the matrix. The scope statement names the entity, the CAGE code, the validated scope, and what falls outside.
  - B. The findings — each should name the failing objective, the evidence examined, what was missing, and a severity **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* The common failure is a finding that states a conclusion. A reader should be able to verify or overturn each finding without redoing the assessment.
  - C. The methodology — it should describe the CAP in general terms
    - *Feedback:* General description is padding. Methodology should state the methods used, the sampling approach and the depth and coverage values applied, and what was examined, tested, or established by interview.
  - D. The recommendation — it should set out a remediation roadmap
    - *Feedback:* It should contain no remediation at all. The recommendation names which of the outcomes the CAP provides for you would recommend.

# Lesson 9 — POA&amp;M, Closeout, the Transfer Test, and What You Do Next
*Day 5 &middot; Afternoon &middot; CAP Phase 4 &middot; Transfer test &middot; Closes the CCP blueprint*

This morning you told Meridian it does not get a certificate. Now you deal with what happens next &mdash; for the company, for the regulatory environment you will work in, and for you. Then you are handed a company you have never seen and given thirty minutes, because that is the only honest measure of whether this week gave you a method or a familiarity.

## Learning objectives
By the end of this session you will be able to:

- **[Construct]** Construct a POA&amp;M consistent with 32 CFR &sect;170.21, distinguishing what may be deferred from what may never be.
- **[Explain]** Explain Conditional versus Final Level 2 status, the 180-day closeout window, and what happens when it expires.

## Key terminology

- **POA&amp;M** — Plan of Action and Milestones. Under 32 CFR §170.21, available only where the assessment score divided by the total number of requirements is at least 0.8, and never for six enumerated requirements.
- **The six** — AC.L2-3.1.20, AC.L2-3.1.22, CA.L2-3.12.4, PE.L2-3.10.3, PE.L2-3.10.4, PE.L2-3.10.5. These must be MET. No score, however high, changes that.
- **Conditional CMMC Status** — Status issued where a POA&M is permitted and in place. Becomes Final on a successful closeout assessment within the window, or expires.
- **Closeout window** — 180 days from the Conditional CMMC Status Date, closed out by a closeout assessment. Miss it and the conditional status expires.
- **Closeout C3PAO** — The firm that conducts the closeout assessment. Need not be the firm that ran Phases 1–3; it assumes responsibility for the Final status determination after its own conflict-of-interest review.
- **Annual affirmation** — Submitted in SPRS each year by the Affirming Official, attesting to continued compliance. A present-tense statement, not a memory of the assessment.
- **Flowdown, tested** — Acuitas Machining — the supplier inside Meridian's own supply chain, and the transfer test.

## 1. What a POA&amp;M can and cannot do

The fact that governs this session

A plan of action is not a way to defer anything you like. Six requirements may never be placed on a
POA&M at all, and a POA&M may not be used at any level unless the assessment score is at least
0.8 of the total.

Two of the six are worth one point each. The cheapest requirements on the sheet are absolute barriers, and no
amount of score makes them go away.

RuleWhat it means in practice

The score thresholdA POA&M may be used only where the assessment score divided by the total number of Level 2 requirements is at least 0.8. For 110 requirements that is 88. Below that there is no plan of action available at all — the answer is no issuance.

The six that may never be deferredAC.L2-3.1.20, AC.L2-3.1.22, CA.L2-3.12.4, PE.L2-3.10.3, PE.L2-3.10.4, PE.L2-3.10.5. These must be MET.

The encryption exceptionSC.L2-3.13.11 may be placed on a POA&M only where encryption is employed but is not FIPS validated. Where no encryption is employed, it may not.

The window180 days from the Conditional CMMC Status Date, closed out by a closeout assessment. Miss it and the conditional status expires.

Who closes it outAn authorized or accredited C3PAO — and it need not be the firm that ran Phases 1 through 3. A closeout C3PAO assumes responsibility for the Final status determination and issues the Final certificate, after its own conflict-of-interest review.

Notice what the point values do not tell you

CA.L2-3.12.4 — the System Security Plan — is worth one point.
PE.L2-3.10.3 — escorting visitors — is worth one point. Both are on the list that may
never be deferred.

A company can be one point from a perfect score and still be unable to obtain any status at all. Scoring weight
measures exploitability; POA&M eligibility measures something else entirely, and conflating the two is the most
expensive mistake available in this area.

## 2. Meridian, as you assessed it

Work Part A from your own findings, not from a supplied list. You will reach the conclusion quickly.

Meridian's assessed score does not approach 88. And two of the six requirements that may never be deferred
are already failing: the System Security Plan requirement, and PE.L2-3.10.3 — the Huntsville warehouse has no
visitor log, no badge access, and delivery drivers come and go.

Meridian is not POA&M-eligible. No CMMC status may issue.

Say the consequence out loud

A determination of no issuance is not the end of the sentence. Write two or three lines on what it means
commercially: the AMCOM recompete the company must win to survive; the clause in the contract it already holds and is
in breach of today; and the affirmation of a score of 88 that its chief executive has already signed, in a federal
system, under penalty.

Assessors do not decide what happens to companies. They do have to be able to say plainly what their
determination means — without softening it, and without enjoying it.

The trap, and it is worth meeting here rather than in the field

The score clears the threshold. 0.836 is comfortably above 0.8, and a company reading only the threshold rule
would expect a Conditional status.

It cannot have one. Two of the six NOT MET requirements are on the never-defer list, and they are the two cheapest
items on the sheet. Both must be MET outright before any status can issue. The score alone never answers the
question.

For each deferrable item the plan needs a milestone, a target date inside the window, and an owner. A milestone
with no owner is a wish. Sequence them so dependencies are visible — baseline configurations before least
privilege, for instance, because you cannot enforce what you have not defined.

And no remediation design. Naming what must be remediated and by when is the plan; specifying how to remediate
it is consulting, and it is still consulting on the last afternoon.

The practical answer to the sustainment problem is continuous monitoring, so that the affirmation reports
something known rather than something assumed. Marcus Ellison affirmed a score of 88 on the basis that other
people told her it was right. Hold that in mind when you write this section.

## 5. Reading a policy change against primary sources

This is the Day 1 exercise returning at full strength. Then you sorted statements from a single alert into fact,
inference, and advice. Now you do it across three sources that do not agree with one another, on a subject where the
primary sources are incomplete and moving.

- Two professional analyses of the same recent CMMC development, from firms that reach different conclusions.

- A government or trade-body notice on the same subject — a different kind of source with a different agenda.

Mark every substantive sentence as stated, inferred, or
advocated. Then the harder question: where the two firms disagree, what would settle it
— and is that thing published, pending, or unknowable?

Why this is the last analytical exercise of the workshop

Everything else you have learned has a shelf life. This is the skill that lets you notice when it expires. An
assessor who can say what would settle a disagreement, and whether that thing exists yet, can tell a client the
difference between a risk and a rumour.

## 6. The transfer test

Thirty minutes. Individual. No notes, no case study, no internet. You receive a profile of a company in
Meridian's supply chain and produce a scope determination.

It is not graded and it does not affect completion. It exists to tell you something you cannot find out any
other way: whether this week gave you a method or a familiarity. Both feel
identical from the inside, right up until you meet a new environment.

Acuitas Machining Inc.

Springfield, Ohio, about 25 miles from Meridian’s Dayton plant. Fourteen people. Precision CNC machining
and inspection — a job shop taking overflow and short-run work. About $800K of its $3.4M revenue comes from a
single relationship: machining overflow on Meridian’s actuator housing line for the AMCOM contract.

No server. No domain. No IT staff. No SSP, no asset inventory, no network diagram, no written security policy of
any kind, and no record of any security decision ever having been made.

The owner's position, stated plainly: “CMMC is a DoD thing — we don't have a contract with the
government, we have a contract with Meridian.”

What you must produce

- Level and authority (~4 min). What level applies and what makes it apply? Answer the owner's
objection directly, in no more than four sentences, and name the instrument you rely on.

- Asset categorization (~14 min). Eight assets into the five categories, one-line basis each.
Where a category is unavailable to Acuitas, say so and say why.

- The consequential unknown (~4 min). The single fact you would most want resolved before
finalizing this scope, why it matters, and what evidence would settle it.

What is being measured

Not recall. Whether the method you have used on one company for four days works on a company you have never
seen — which is the only thing that would make you useful to the next OSC that hires you.

A confident wrong categorization scores worse than a correctly hedged one. If a category is genuinely arguable,
say what makes it arguable and what would settle it. That is the assessor's answer.

## ThreadBridge — Close it out, then prove the method transfers

The POA&M Builder runs Parts A to C. Part A takes your own assessed score and findings and
walks the eligibility test — the threshold, then the never-defer list — and asks you to state the
commercial consequence in your own words. Part B loads the revised assessment at 92 and lets you build the plan
item by item; the tool refuses a deferral of CA.L2-3.12.4 or PE.L2-3.10.3 and tells you why. Part C is closeout and
sustainment.

Then the Acuitas transfer test opens cold, on a thirty-minute timer, with no access to the
Meridian material. Eight assets, three questions. When the timer stops, the determination key opens —
including the honest account of where the test is won or lost, which is the industrial printer controller.

Nothing anywhere in D9 tells Meridian how to remediate anything. Naming what must be done and by when is
the plan. Designing it is somebody else's job — and after this week, never yours on a company you assess.

## Knowledge check

**Q1.** An OSC scores 92 of 110 (0.836). Its NOT MET requirements include CA.L2-3.12.4 and PE.L2-3.10.3. What status may issue?
  - A. Conditional Level 2 — the score clears the 0.8 threshold
    - *Feedback:* The trap this session exists to spring. Clearing the threshold is necessary and not sufficient.
  - B. None — two NOT MET requirements may never be placed on a POA&M **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* Both must be MET outright before any status can issue. They are the two cheapest items on the sheet, at one point each. The score alone never answers the question.
  - C. Final Level 2, since the deductions are minor
    - *Feedback:* Final status requires all requirements MET, and these are not.
  - D. Conditional Level 2, with the two ineligible items deferred by exception
    - *Feedback:* There is no exception mechanism. The list is absolute.

**Q2.** Which requirement may be placed on a POA&M only in a specific circumstance?
  - A. CA.L2-3.12.4, where the SSP is in draft
    - *Feedback:* 3.12.4 may never be placed on a POA&M in any circumstance.
  - B. SC.L2-3.13.11, where encryption is employed but is not FIPS validated **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* Where no encryption is employed at all, it may not be deferred. Note the symmetry with the scoring exception: the same distinction drives both the 3-versus-5 deduction and POA&M eligibility.
  - C. PE.L2-3.10.4, where the facility is leased
    - *Feedback:* On the never-defer list, and tenancy is irrelevant.
  - D. AC.L2-3.1.20, where the external system is government-owned
    - *Feedback:* Also on the never-defer list.

**Q3.** From what date does the 180-day closeout window run?
  - A. The date of the out-brief
    - *Feedback:* The out-brief is a Phase 3 event and does not start the clock.
  - B. The Conditional CMMC Status Date **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* 180 days from that date, closed out by a closeout assessment. Miss it and the conditional status expires.
  - C. The date the POA&M is submitted
    - *Feedback:* Submission is not the trigger.
  - D. The date the Contracting Officer accepts the score
    - *Feedback:* No such acceptance step exists in the closeout mechanics.

**Q4.** Who may conduct the closeout assessment?
  - A. Only the C3PAO that conducted Phases 1 through 3
    - *Feedback:* A common assumption and the rule is explicitly the opposite.
  - B. Any authorized or accredited C3PAO, after its own conflict-of-interest review **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* The closeout firm assumes responsibility for the Final status determination and issues the Final certificate. Its conflict review is its own, not inherited.
  - C. DIBCAC
    - *Feedback:* DIBCAC conducts government-led assessments, including Level 3. Closeout of a Level 2 conditional status is C3PAO work.
  - D. The OSC, by self-assessment
    - *Feedback:* A conditional status arising from a certification assessment cannot be closed out by self-assessment.

**Q5.** Acuitas's owner says CMMC does not apply because he has no contract with the Government. What is the complete answer?
  - A. He is right for now, but Meridian may impose requirements commercially
    - *Feedback:* This concedes the owner's premise. The obligation is not a commercial courtesy.
  - B. Level 2 applies: he receives CTI extracted from the TDP, through a subcontract incorporating DFARS 252.204-7012, which flows down under paragraph (m) **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* The obligation follows the information, not the tier. The clause is in his subcontract, flowdown is how it got there, Meridian was required to put it there, and whether he has read it is irrelevant to whether it binds him.
  - C. Level 1 applies, because subcontractors carry the lower tier
    - *Feedback:* The most serious error available on this test, and it is the owner's own error. Tier does not determine level; information does.
  - D. Level 2 applies because he handles CUI
    - *Feedback:* The right conclusion without the authority. Naming the flowdown mechanism is what makes it an answer.

**Q6.** Acuitas's three CNC machining centres have embedded Windows controllers that retain drawing-derived files for months. How are they categorized?
  - A. Out of scope — they are machine tools
    - *Feedback:* The weakest answer. They have not been read as information systems, and they retain CUI for months.
  - B. A full CUI Asset, assessed against all 110 requirements
    - *Feedback:* Thorough, and it misses that the Specialized Asset category exists and covers Operational Technology.
  - C. Specialized Asset by type — but the treatment is conditional on documentation and risk-based policies Acuitas cannot produce, so they stay in the assessment **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* Where the test is won or lost. The category exists and Acuitas cannot claim it: no inventory, no SSP, no network diagram, no policy of any kind. Knowing the rule and its condition is the difference between having read the scoping guide and being able to use it.
  - D. Contractor Risk Managed, since machining is incidental to the CUI
    - *Feedback:* That category carries the same documentation and policy condition, which also fails — and OT is the better-fitting type in any case.

**Q7.** Acuitas's part-time bookkeeper works from home on her own computer with access to the full shared drive. How is she treated?
  - A. Out of scope — she handles accounting, not CUI
    - *Feedback:* The dismissal the test is designed to catch. Her access is to the full shared drive, which holds the pattern files.
  - B. An External Service Provider handling CUI, assessed inside the OSC assessment, with a CRM required **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* She holds an account with access to CUI and works from her own equipment. No CRM exists. Note the second ESP alongside her: the IT contractor with tenant administrator rights, who handles Security Protection Data.
  - C. A Contractor Risk Managed Asset
    - *Feedback:* Categories apply to assets. An external party is analysed under the ESP rule.
  - D. An employee for scoping purposes, since she has an account
    - *Feedback:* Having an account does not make an external party internal. The ESP rule exists precisely for this.

**Q8.** At Acuitas, what should be scoped out?
  - A. The CMM inspection station, since it only measures parts
    - *Feedback:* It holds inspection programs written from the Meridian drawings and generates reports referencing them. Students who scope the machine tools carefully and forget the metrology station have found the obvious OT and missed the quieter one.
  - B. The commercial customers' artwork folders
    - *Feedback:* Folders are not assets, and they sit on a shared drive every account can read.
  - C. Nothing — on a single flat wireless network with a shared drive every account can read, no justification of inability exists **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* Out-of-Scope requires the contractor to be prepared to justify the asset's inability to process, store, or transmit CUI. The absence of segmentation is what makes that impossible, and saying so explicitly is the correct answer.
  - D. The production PC, since it only sends print jobs
    - *Feedback:* It opens pattern files. It is a CUI Asset, and the shared login with the password on the monitor is a finding rather than a scoping question.

**Q9.** Two law firm analyses of the same CMMC development reach different conclusions. What is the assessor's most useful next question?
  - A. Which firm has more CMMC experience?
    - *Feedback:* Authority of the author is not how a disagreement about a primary source is settled.
  - B. What would settle the disagreement, and is that thing published, pending, or unknowable? **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* The question that distinguishes a risk from a rumour. An assessor who can answer it can tell a client which of the two they are looking at.
  - C. Which conclusion is more conservative?
    - *Feedback:* Conservatism is a posture, not an analysis, and it can be wrong in either direction.
  - D. What does the CMMC PMO say publicly?
    - *Feedback:* Useful input, and it does not resolve a disagreement about what the existing instruments mean.

**Q10.** What does the annual affirmation attest, and who may make it?
  - A. That the assessment was conducted correctly; the Lead CCA
    - *Feedback:* The affirmation is the OSC's statement about itself, not the assessor's about the assessment.
  - B. That the organization continues to meet the requirements; a senior official with authority to bind the OSC **(correct)**
    - *Feedback:* A present-tense statement, not a memory of the assessment. The practical answer is continuous monitoring, so that it reports something known rather than assumed. False Claims Act exposure attaches personally to the signer.
  - C. That no incidents occurred in the preceding year; the IT Manager
    - *Feedback:* Wrong on both counts. Incidents are reported under a separate clause, and the IT Manager cannot bind the organization.
  - D. That the POA&M has been closed; the closeout C3PAO
    - *Feedback:* Closeout is a separate mechanism with its own assessment, and the C3PAO does not affirm on the OSC's behalf.

# Bonus — Teaching CMMC &mdash; A Faculty and Facilitator Guide
*Faculty PD &middot; Not part of the 5-day sequence*

You have worked the material. This is how to deploy it: the 8-week graded version it was derived from, the facilitation decisions that make or break each session, the answer keys and what to do with them, the instruments that must be withheld until issued, and the maintenance obligation that comes with teaching a subject in active regulatory flux.

## Learning objectives
This guide is for the person delivering the workshop. By the end of it you will be able to:

- **[Choose]** Choose between the 5-day workshop, the 8-week graded course, and the hybrid, on the basis of cohort and contact hours.
- **[Facilitate]** Facilitate each session's highest-risk moments, including the ones where the correct answer is uncomfortable.
- **[Assess]** Assess against the competency rubric rather than a grade, and know what Competent versus Assessor-Ready looks like.
- **[Protect]** Protect the instruments that must be issued cold, and know why each one is withheld.
- **[Maintain]** Maintain the material against a regulatory position that is actively moving.

## Key terminology

- **Competency rubric** — Not Yet / Developing / Competent / Assessor-Ready. Competent is the completion standard; Assessor-Ready describes work that would survive professional challenge.
- **Cold instrument** — Material that must not be released in advance: the Module 7 evidence package, the interview role cards, and the Acuitas transfer test.
- **Challenge round** — The peer-challenge format used for scoping and domain determinations. Arbitrated against the text on screen, never by seniority.
- **Three-step fade** — Watch a worked example, then work with support, then work unsupported. The evidence sessions are built on it.
- **Minimum viable cohort** — Twelve students — four teams of three — for the split-case design. Below that, run a single case and extend the challenge rounds.

## 2. The facilitation decisions that matter

Teach the test before the name. Do the two conditional categories last and slowly: read the condition aloud, then ask what Meridian would show you. The silence is the lesson.

L6Cross-presentation
Require teams to lead with their least comfortable determination. Teams instinctively present their best work, which wastes the exercise — nobody learns from a determination everyone agrees with.

Poll the room before revealing. Expect a substantial minority at 3. Work the conjunction on screen: remote and privileged. The spread is the lesson.

Play it once per team, late, when they are tired and want to be helpful. A team that answers has failed the exercise regardless of their determinations — and that failure teaches more than the finding would have.

Ten minutes, immediately, while the reasoning is live. Poll on the printer controller before revealing anything — students remember their own wrong answer better than your right one. Then ask who scoped something out and make them defend it. On that environment nobody should have.

## 3. What must be withheld

Three cold instruments

The Day 5 evidence package. Issued at the in-brief. A team that has read the nine documents in
advance has done a different exercise, and the time pressure that makes the mock assessment realistic disappears.

The interview role cards. Role-players only, one card each, never shown to assessment teams.
Every card contains at least one fact that contradicts something in the evidence package, and those contradictions
are the assessable content.

The Acuitas transfer test. Do not release the profile in advance, do not preview the company,
and do not answer questions about it once the clock starts.

How to play the interview cards

The rule that makes it work: answer the question you were asked, and nothing more. Each card has
material that is volunteered freely and material that emerges only if a student asks a question that reaches it
— a real question, not a yes-or-no invitation to agree.

If a student asks “you review the logs regularly, correct?”, say yes. That is a true answer to a bad
question, and the student learning why it was a bad question is the point of the exercise. If a team asks only
closed questions, let the interview end early and uselessly. The silence afterwards teaches more than a rescue
would.

Hold the contradictions. If a team leaves without finding any of them, that is the finding — and it is the
one to open the debrief with.

## 4. Assessing competency rather than grading

Four levels, applied to every deliverable. Competent is the completion standard;
Assessor-Ready describes work that would survive professional challenge.

LevelWhat it looks like across the deliverables

Not YetAdopts the contractor's own claims wholesale. Categorizes by intuition or by what an asset is for. Determines at the requirement level. Recommends remediation.

DevelopingHandles the obvious cases and goes quiet on the ambiguous ones. Applies one test but not both. Names a requirement but not the objective. Advice appears once, in passing.

CompetentEvery determination evidenced and traceable. Conditional categories recognised as earned. Both adequacy and sufficiency applied. No advice anywhere; gaps named without fixes.

Assessor-ReadySays what would change each answer. Catches the under-classified item and explains why that direction is more dangerous. Names a gap so precisely that the fix is obvious without ever being stated. Written so that a company that dislikes the result still recognises it as fair.

The single most useful marking habit

Look for the what would change it clause. A determination that cannot describe its own falsifying
evidence has not been reasoned — it has been asserted. That one test separates Competent from Developing more
reliably than any other, and it is fast to apply.
