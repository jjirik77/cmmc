Day 1 · Afternoon · CCP Domains 1 & 3

The Compliance Landscape and the CMMC Ecosystem

Cybersecurity standards do not become obligations on their own. Someone writes one, a government adopts it, and a contracting officer puts it in a contract. Until that happens, a standard is advice. This session is about how CMMC stopped being advice — and about reading a regulatory disruption against primary sources instead of headlines.

Learning objectives

By the end of this session you will be able to:

  • TraceTrace the regulatory chain from Executive Order 13556 through 32 CFR Part 170 and the 48 CFR acquisition rule to DFARS 252.204-7021 appearing in a solicitation.
  • DistinguishDistinguish a voluntary framework from a mandatory, assessed, contractually enforced requirement set.

1

Why a cybersecurity standard became a contract term

NIST SP 800-171 was published in 2015 and revised in 2020. On its own it binds nobody. It is a NIST publication: a specification of 110 security requirements for protecting CUI on nonfederal systems, with no enforcement mechanism of any kind.

What made it consequential was a clause. DFARS 252.204-7012 took a NIST document and made implementing it a term of a Department of Defense contract. From that moment a contractor that failed to implement 800-171 was not falling short of best practice — it was in breach of contract.

CMMC is the layer that came next, and it answers a different question. 7012 said you must implement this. It did not say and someone will check. Fifteen years of self-attestation produced a defense industrial base in which the attestations and the reality had drifted a long way apart. CMMC is the verification regime built on top of an obligation that already existed.

Why this matters for the rest of the week

Hold the two apart. The obligation to protect CUI lives in DFARS 252.204-7012 and dates from 2017. The verification of that obligation lives in 32 CFR Part 170 and DFARS 252.204-7021 and dates from 2024–2025. The two are separable, and separating them is the single most useful analytical move available in this field — you will use it again on Friday.

2

The chain, link by link

This is the sequence that turns a NIST publication into something that can cost a company a contract. The order and the joins are what matter.

#LinkWhat it does
1EO 13556 (2010)Creates the government-wide CUI Program and names NARA as Executive Agent. Before this, agencies invented their own markings.
232 CFR Part 2002 (2016)Implements the CUI Program: who may designate CUI, how it is marked, safeguarded, disseminated, and decontrolled.
3NIST SP 800-171 Rev 2 (2020)Specifies 110 security requirements for protecting CUI on nonfederal systems. Binding on no one by itself.
4DFARS 252.204-7012 (2017)Makes 800-171 contractual for DoD contractors, and adds 72-hour cyber incident reporting.
5DFARS 252.204-7019 / -7020Require a current DoD Assessment score posted in SPRS as a condition of award.
632 CFR Part 170 (eff. Dec 2024)Establishes the CMMC Program: three levels, assessment types, scoping rules, affirmation, the ecosystem.
748 CFR acquisition rule (eff. Nov 2025)Amends the DFARS so contracting officers may insert the CMMC clause into solicitations and contracts.
8DFARS 252.204-7021The clause. Makes a specific CMMC level and status a condition of award and of continued performance.
9FlowdownThe prime passes the requirement to subcontractors at the level appropriate to the information each one handles.
Exam tip

Steps 1 through 5 were in force years before CMMC existed. That matters every time someone argues that a change to the CMMC programme has changed what a contractor owes: the obligation to protect CUI lives at step 4, not at step 8. You will meet that argument in the field. You should be able to dismantle it in one sentence.

3

The DFARS clause family, and what each one does

Four clauses appear together in DoD solicitations and are routinely confused with one another. They do different work and they were added at different times.

ClauseTitleWhat it actually requires
FAR 52.204-21Basic Safeguarding of Covered Contractor Information Systems Fifteen basic safeguarding requirements where the contractor handles FCI. The floor for almost every federal contract.
DFARS 252.204-7008Compliance with Safeguarding Covered Defense Information Controls A representation at proposal time that the offeror will implement 800-171, and the route by which a deviation can be requested.
DFARS 252.204-7012Safeguarding CDI and Cyber Incident Reporting Adequate security via 800-171 Rev 2; the 72-hour report; malicious software submission; 90-day media preservation; DoD access; cloud provider requirements; flowdown.
DFARS 252.204-7019 / -7020NIST SP 800-171 DoD Assessment Requirements A current assessment of record in SPRS as a condition of award, and DoD access to conduct higher-level assessments.
DFARS 252.204-7021Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification Requirements A specific CMMC level and status as a condition of award and of continued performance. The clause CMMC actually rides on.
4

Reading a solicitation: where the level actually comes from

Solicitation W58RGZ-26-R-0301 — the AMCOM recompete Meridian must win — carries DFARS 252.204-7021 with the required CMMC status left as a fill-in, and points to Section L. Section L says the status “shall be that which corresponds to the categories of information identified in Section C, determined in accordance with 32 CFR Part 170.”

In other words: the solicitation does not tell you. You have to reason it out. The reasoning runs through the Statement of Work, not the clause list.

What Section C says

  • C.3.2 — on award the Government furnishes the complete Technical Data Package: engineering drawings, pattern grading specifications, seam and stitch construction standards, flame-resistance test protocols, the approved materials list. Portions bear Distribution Statement D and are subject to export control provisions cited in Section I.
  • C.3.3 — the contractor generates and transmits production status reports, first-article test results, lot traceability records, and deviation requests, referencing drawing numbers and revision levels from the TDP.
  • C.4.1 — any subcontractor receiving any portion of the TDP must be identified to the Contracting Officer, and the safeguarding and certification requirements of Section I must be included in that subcontract.

Technical information with military application, subject to controls on access, use, reproduction, modification, release, or dissemination, is Controlled Technical Information — a CUI category. CUI drives Level 2. C.3.3 matters independently: information the contractor generates during performance is captured too. And C.4.1 is flowdown, stated in the Statement of Work before it appears in any clause.

Exam tip

Candidates look for the level in the clause list and find a blank. The level is determined by the information, under 32 CFR Part 170, and the Statement of Work is where the information is described. Read Section C before Section I.

5

Policy announcement versus regulation

CMMC is an active programme, and active programmes generate announcements: memoranda, press releases, industry days, testimony, and a steady stream of professional commentary interpreting all of it. Some of that changes what a contractor owes. Most of it does not, and telling the two apart is a skill with a longer shelf life than any particular announcement.

Three instruments can change what is required
  • A class deviation — a formal acquisition instrument changing how a clause is applied across a class of contracts.
  • A DFARS or FAR rule change — amending the clause text itself.
  • An amendment to 32 CFR Part 170 — changing the programme rule.

Until one of those issues, the framework is what the published instruments say it is. A memorandum announcing an intention, a schedule adjustment, a pilot, or a review does not amend a regulation, however consequential it may turn out to be. Neither does a recommendation to an agency official, and neither does a press report about either.

What you are readingDoes it change the requirement?What to do with it
An amendment to 32 CFR Part 170YesRead it; re-derive anything downstream that depended on the old text
A DFARS or FAR rule changeYesRead the new clause text, not the summary of it
A class deviationYes, for the class it namesCheck whether the contract in front of you is in that class
A policy memorandum or announcementNot by itselfNote it; watch for the instrument that would implement it
A task force report or recommendationNoTrack it as a leading indicator, nothing more
A law firm alert or vendor briefingNoUse it to stay current; never cite it
Why an assessor in particular needs this

Because the question arrives as a request. A contractor reads that something has changed, concludes that an obligation has lifted, and asks you to agree. Your answer is not a matter of opinion or of how the news was reported — it is whether an instrument issued, and what its text says.

The same discipline protects you in the other direction. An announcement that sounds alarming does not create an obligation either, and an assessor who writes findings against requirements that were never enacted is as wrong as one who ignores requirements that were.

Note also that obligations rarely move together. The chain in the previous section has nine links from four different bodies of law, enacted across fifteen years. A change at one link frequently leaves the others exactly where they were — which is why “has CMMC changed?” is almost never a question with a single answer, and why the useful question is always which instrument, and what does its text now say?

6

Fact, inference, advice

You are entering a field with a very large amount of published commentary and a very small amount of governing text. The discipline that keeps you current without making you wrong is the ability to read a professional alert and sort every sentence into three buckets.

BucketWhat it isExample shapeCitable?
StatedWhat the primary source actually says “32 CFR 170.21 permits a POA&M only where the score is at least 0.8 of the total.”Yes — cite the source, not the alert
InferredWhat the author concludes from it “This suggests the Department intends to ease the burden on small suppliers.”No
AdvocatedWhat the author recommends the reader do “Contractors should begin readiness work now rather than waiting.”No

All three are legitimate and useful. Conflating them is not. A finding that rests on a law firm's inference is a finding that collapses the moment someone reads the underlying memorandum.

The habit worth building this week

When commentary tells you something surprising, find the primary source that supports it before you accept it. On Friday you will do this across three sources that disagree with one another, on a subject where the primary sources are incomplete and moving. It is the last analytical exercise of the workshop because it is the one that keeps everything else current after it stops being true.

7

Source authority, ranked

In an assessment, and on the exams, the correct answer is the one the governing document supports. Ranking sources by authority is itself a professional skill.

TierCategoryRule
1Governing law and regulation (CFR, FAR, DFARS)Controlling. Cite this when a requirement is disputed. It is the law.
2Official CMMC program documents (DoW CIO)Controlling for assessment practice. The Assessment and Scoping Guides govern how a requirement is assessed.
3NIST standards and guidanceIncorporated by reference. 800-171 Rev 2 and 800-171A define the requirements and objectives.
4Ecosystem governance (Cyber AB, ISACA/CAICO)Controlling for assessor conduct. The CAP and CoPC govern behaviour; ISACA governs the credentials.
5CUI governance (NARA, Executive Order)Controlling for CUI identification. Contractors do not self-designate.
6Practitioner and legal analysisCurrent-awareness only. Never citable as a requirement.
7Training material, portals, case studiesInstructional. Useful for practice; never authority.

Note where this workshop sits: tier 7. When it and a governing document disagree, the governing document wins.

Meridian · Day 1 Afternoon · Deliverable D1

Trace the clause chain to the contract

You have the solicitation. Now build the chain that binds Meridian to it, and be able to hand the result to Marcus Ellison — the chief executive, who is capable, busy, and not a compliance specialist.

The Clause Trace tool walks you through the nine links, then asks you three questions:

  • What legal and regulatory authority makes CMMC applicable to this solicitation?
  • What CMMC level does the offeror need, and what evidence of that level must exist at the time of award?
  • Which obligations arise independently of CMMC itself — that is, which would still bind this company if the CMMC clause were struck from the contract tomorrow?

Cite primary sources only — the CFR, the FAR, the DFARS, or an official CMMC document, named to the part, section, or clause. You may have used a law firm alert to orient yourself; it does not appear in your answer.

The tool checks your chain against the authorities, shows you a model answer once you have committed to your own, and prints to PDF as your D1 Regulatory Trace Memo.
